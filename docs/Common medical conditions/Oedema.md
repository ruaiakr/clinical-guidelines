<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Oedema – Adult/Child</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p>Oedema is the accumulation of fluid in the tissues, usually seen as swelling. It may be generalised or localised. Oedema can be a sign of underlying systemic disease and must be evaluated carefully. Bilateral oedema often suggests systemic causes such as cardiac, renal, or hepatic disease, while unilateral oedema may point to local causes such as infection or thrombosis.</p>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Consider serious causes such as heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, renal failure, liver disease, or malnutrition</li>
<li>In pregnant women, assess for pre-eclampsia</li>
<li>Swelling of one leg may be due to local causes including infection, injury, or vein thrombosis</li>
<li>Refer significant oedema for review at central hospital</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Swelling of both legs, face, abdomen, or eyelids</li>
<li>Shortness of breath, fatigue, distended neck veins (suggesting heart failure)</li>
<li>Jaundice or ascites (suggesting liver disease)</li>
<li>Pallor, tiredness, dizziness (suggesting anaemia)</li>
<li>Swelling of arms and legs with skin changes in children (suggesting Kwashiorkor)</li>
<li>Ankle swelling in pregnancy (physiological or pre-eclampsia)</li>
<li>Swelling of only one leg – may be due to cellulitis, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), or injury</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Check vital signs and oxygen saturation</li>
<li>Triage and prioritise if patient is breathless, confused, or acutely unwell</li>
<li>In pregnant women with oedema + raised BP or proteinuria, refer urgently for suspected pre-eclampsia</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Full history including:
<ul>
<li>Onset, location, progression of swelling</li>
<li>Symptoms of heart, kidney or liver disease</li>
<li>Medication history</li>
<li>Dietary status</li>
<li>Pregnancy status</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Physical examination:
<ul>
<li>Assess site and extent of oedema</li>
<li>Look for signs of heart failure (e.g. crepitations, elevated JVP)</li>
<li>Examine liver and abdomen for signs of liver disease or ascites</li>
<li>Check BP and urine (for protein) in pregnant women</li>
<li>Look for local signs of infection or thrombosis in one leg</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">4. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Manage according to likely cause:
<ul>
<li>Heart failure: consider diuretics if advised by MO</li>
<li>Nephrotic syndrome or renal failure: consult MO; may require fluid/salt restriction</li>
<li>Liver disease: supportive care; refer</li>
<li>Kwashiorkor: nutritional rehabilitation</li>
<li>Pregnancy: assess for pre-eclampsia</li>
<li>Infection/DVT in one leg: initiate antibiotics if cellulitis; refer if thrombosis suspected</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Advise rest with leg elevation where appropriate</li>
<li>Monitor urine output and vital signs</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Monitor oedema and underlying cause</li>
<li>Adjust treatment based on clinical course</li>
<li>Reassess nutritional status in children or malnourished adults</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">6. Referral/consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Refer to central hospital if:
<ul>
<li>Generalised oedema with signs of systemic illness</li>
<li>No improvement</li>
<li>Pregnant woman with high BP or proteinuria</li>
<li>Unexplained or worsening one-leg oedema</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</div>
