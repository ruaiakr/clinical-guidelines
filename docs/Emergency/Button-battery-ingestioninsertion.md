# Button battery ingestion/insertion - Adult/Child

Button battery ingestion/insertion -
Adult/Child
Recommend 1,2
•	 A suspected or proven battery ingestion is a time critical emergency - urgent
x-ray needed
•	 Suspect button battery ingestion in all children &lt; 5 years of age with non-specific
symptoms
Background 1,2
•	 Button (disc) batteries cause burns resulting in life-threatening injuries
•	 Failure to remove from the oesophagus within 2 hours can lead to perforation of
the
•	 oesophagus, fistula (eg oesophageal-aortal/tracheal) and catastrophic
haemorrhage
•	 Oral honey may slow the development of a chemical burn for a swallowed button
battery3
1.
May present with 1,2,4
•	 Battery missing
•	 Seen to have been playing with battery
•	 Self reported by child (older)
•	 Gagging, gulp, cough or choking episode:

–
may be overheard when child swallows battery rather than directly observed
•	 Child may deny ingestion
•	 Very non-specific symptoms:

–
unexplained partial food refusal - may still take soft food/fluids

–
drooling or regurgitation

–
croup like cough

–
chest pain

–
upper GI bleeding (melaena/black stools; haematemesis - may mimic
epistaxis):

–
may be small bleed initially, then catastrophic bleed

–
fever/vomiting/signs of infection without a clear focus

–
pain/grunting

–
Bloody discharge from ear, nose, vagina, rectum - may have inserted battery

2.	 Immediate management
•	 If inhaled, see Choking
•	 If haematemesis or melaena:

–
insert IVC x 2

–
get rapid history

–
urgently consult MO
•	 Do vital signs
If ingestion (swallowed) suspected
•	 Urgently contact MO:

–
if no x-ray facilities, we will need urgent evacuation for x-ray
•	 If the x-ray shows battery is located in the hypopharynx (bottom of throat) or
oesophagus:5

–
URGENT evacuation for removal via endoscopy required
•	 Give oral honey if ingestion suspected &lt; 12 hours and:3,6

–
child ≥ 12 months old

–
able to swallow

–
give 10 mL (2 teaspoons) every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses (or as guided by
MO)
•	 keep nil by mouth otherwise
•	 If the battery is in the stomach or beyond:2

–
management will depend on factors such as age of child, symptoms, size of
battery, time of suspected ingestion

–
MO will advise
•	 Monitor vital signs
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Ask about - if known:

–
timing of ingestion

–
size of battery (if ≥ 2 cm, more likely to lodge in oesophagus)

–
was ingestion witnessed

–
what treatment (if any) has been tried at home

–
colour of stools, any blood in vomit, or any other signs/symptoms
4.	 Management
•	 Continue management of suspected swallowed battery in collaboration with MO
•	 If insertion suspected eg into vagina, ear, rectum, nose:

–
consult MO regarding urgent removal

–
x-ray may be required if unexplained nasal/ear/vaginal discharge and unable
to visualise cause1
