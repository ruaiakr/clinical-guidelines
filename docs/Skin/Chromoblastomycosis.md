# Chromoblastomycosis

Head Lice: (Pediculosis)
The head louse is an obligate human parasite that lives exclusively on scalp hair.
Transmission occurs mostly via direct head-to-head contact. The head louse can
survive up to 4 days on fomites such as brushes, barrettes, and headgear; therefore,
indirect transmission is possible.
Commonly seen in school age children- particularly girls.
1.
Immediate management Not applicable
2.	 Clinical assessment
Direct observation of adult lice and egg capsules (nits) is diagnostic:
•	 Adult lice are small, wingless ectoparasites measuring 1-3 mm long with
elongated bodies and 3 pairs of claw-like legs.
•	 Nits (ovoid eggs about the size of a sesame seed) appear as 0.5- to 1-mm, gray-
white specks that are firmly attached to individual hair shafts, typically located
within a centimeter of the scalp.
Excoriations, erythema, scaling, and/or papular urticaria may be present within the
scalp, behind the ears, and along the neck as a result of a hypersensitivity reaction
to live lice and resulting intense pruritus. Cervical and occipital lymph nodes may
swell because of secondary bacterial infection.
Pyoderma in the scalp or on the posterior neck along with occipital and cervical
lymphadenopathy should raise the suspicion for possible pediculosis infestation.
3.	 Management
Successful treatment requires elimination of all adult lice and eggs. This can be
accomplished via manual removal of nits and lice, such as with a nit comb, or death
of the active lice and nits.
Pharmacological
•	 Permethrin 1% or 5%, apply on head for 8-12hrs then rinse with warm water.
•	 Infected- Give antibiotic- flucloxacillin (according to age and weight) or septrin if
allergies to penicillin.

Chromoblastomycosis
is a subcutaneous fungal infection caused by one of several dematiaceous
(pigmented) fungi. Chromoblastomycosis is officially recognized by the World
Health Organization (WHO) as a neglected tropical disease (NTD).
•	 The fungus is found in soil, plants, and decaying wood. Splinters and traumatic
penetration are common sources of inoculation, occurring about 1-2 months
before the development of skin lesions
•	 It is typically found on the lower extremities of middle-aged men in rural areas of
tropical and subtropical climates.
•	 It is a locally progressive disease, usually evolving very slowly over many years
without many associated symptoms. Early on, there may be an erythematous
papule or a warty plaque. This gradually enlarges if untreated and may become
annular, vegetating, or nodular. Lymphatic and cutaneous dissemination have
been described
1.
Management
Therapy options include oral antifungal agents, depending on sensitivities, alone or
in combination with cryotherapy:
•	 Itraconazole 200-400 mg by mouth daily for 6-12 months (with or without
flucytosine 30 mg/kg 4 times daily), or
•	 Terbinafine 500-1000 mg by mouth daily for 6-12 months (efficacy of lower
doses [250 mg/day] has also been reported), or
•	 Posaconazole 800 mg/day (good efficacy and tolerance, but high cost).
2.	 Alternative possible treatments:
•	 Refer for Cryotherapy with liquid nitrogen has a 40% cure rate when used as
monotherapy (Not applicable in Outer islands)
•	 Heat therapy with pocket warmers 24 hours/day for a couple of months.
•	 Surgery should only be considered for limited or refractory lesions due to
concern for dissemination with manipulation of the disease.
•	 Topical application of imiquimod has shown marked improvement in lesions,
either alone or combined with oral antifungal therapy.
