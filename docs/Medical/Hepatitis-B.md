Hepatitis B – Adult/Child
Recommend1
•	 Acute hepatitis B is a notifiable condition and a vaccine-preventable disease
•	 Screen all patients who test positive for an STI for hepatitis B virus (HBV)
•	 Screening for HBV, HIV, and syphilis should be offered to all antenatal mothers
during initial antenatal care (ANC) clinic
•	 Use WHO pre-qualified point-of-care rapid diagnostic tests for screening, e.g.,
Determine™ HBsAg2 test
•	 HBV-positive individuals must be referred to local clinics and linked to hepatitis
care services
•	 All neonates must be given timely HBV birth dose vaccination within 24 hours of
birth
•	 Advise not to share razors, toothbrushes, unsafe tattooing tools or any blood-
contaminated personal items
Background1,2
•	 Hepatitis B is endemic in Kiribati
•	 Acute HBV infection is often asymptomatic:

–
50–70% of adults and up to 90% of children have subclinical infection
•	 Risk of chronic infection decreases with age:

–
~90% of infants infected at birth develop chronic HBV

–
&lt;10% of adults do
•	 Up to 25% of people with chronic HBV may develop cirrhosis, liver failure, or
hepatocellular carcinoma
•	 Transmission routes:

–
Perinatal (mother-to-child) during child-birth or obstetric interventions

–
Percutaneous (e.g. injecting drug use)

–
Sexual contact
•	 Patients with acute HBV infection require follow-up HbsAg testing after 6
months
1.
May present with1,3
•	 No symptoms
•	 If present, general symptoms are:

–
Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain

–
Loss of appetite

–
Rash, low-grade fever

–
Jaundice (yellow skin/eyes), dark urine, pale stools
•	 Laboratory findings:

–
HBsAg positive (in a person previously negative)

2.	 Immediate management
•	 HBV is a notifiable disease and should be reported to the National Hepatitis
Program
•	 Acute infection is usually self-limiting and can be managed as an out-patient
case
•	 Counselling and education of patient and immediate family of condition,
transmission, treatment options and prevention strategies
3.	 Clinical assessment2,3
•	 History:

–
Mode of exposure: unsafe sex, injecting drugs, shared equipment, tattoos

–
Family history of liver disease or HBV

–
Vaccination history

–
In pregnant patients: gestation, antenatal care attendance
•	 Examination:

–
Vitals (Q-ADDS/CEWT)

–
Check for liver tenderness, hepatomegaly, ascites, or history of haematemesis/
melena
•	 Investigations:

–
HBsAg

–
+/- Hepatitis A, C, D serology (discuss with Hepatitis Program)

–
LFTs, INR – calculate APRI-score
•	 In antenatal patients:

–
Initiate antiviral prophylaxis at 24 weeks (or earlier if advised) for all HBsAg-
positive mothers

–
Outer island mothers should begin treatment early and remain on therapy
post-delivery

–
Continue antiviral treatment until at least 6 weeks postpartum
