<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)</h1>
<p class="pccm-page-subtitle">Also see <a href="/docs/Emergency/Diabetic-ketoacidosis">Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)</a></p>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<ul>
<li>Can be rapidly fatal. Avoid delays to treatment - IV fluids, potassium replacement + IV insulin<sup>1,2</sup></li>
<li>Be aware - excessive thirst + frequent urination can be a sign of undiagnosed diabetes</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<ul>
<li>DKA - BGL &gt; 11 + acidosis, sudden onset<sup>1,3</sup> — see <a href="/docs/Emergency/Diabetic-ketoacidosis">DKA</a></li>
<li>HHS - BGL &gt; 33.3 + profound dehydration without ketosis - gradual onset, commonly caused by acute illness<sup>1</sup></li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">HHS — definition and differences from DKA</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>HHS:</strong> BGL &gt; 33.3 + profound dehydration without ketosis; gradual onset; commonly precipitated by acute illness<sup>1</sup></li>
<li><strong>DKA:</strong> BGL &gt; 11 + acidosis; sudden onset<sup>1,3</sup></li>
<li>Key differences:
<ul>
<li>HHS has marked hyperglycaemia and dehydration <strong>without</strong> significant ketosis/acidosis</li>
<li>DKA features acidosis ± ketones; may have Kussmaul breathing and fruity (acetone) breath</li>
<li>HHS develops more gradually; DKA tends to present more acutely</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>For full DKA-focused presentation, assessment and management guide, see <a href="/docs/Emergency/Diabetic-ketoacidosis">Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)</a></li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with<sup>4</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>↑BGL (typically very high — HHS &gt; 33.3)</li>
<li>Excessive thirst, dehydration (often profound in HHS)</li>
<li>Confusion, ↓LOC or unresponsive</li>
<li>Abdominal pain ± vomiting may occur</li>
<li><strong>Note:</strong> Kussmaul breathing and fruity acetone breath are more typical of DKA than HHS</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2. Immediate management<sup>2,3</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>DRSABCD</li>
<li>Rapidly assess + manage concurrently</li>
<li>Do vital signs</li>
<li>Insert IVC x 2 into large veins</li>
<li>Start IV fluids - sodium chloride 0.9%
<ul>
<li>adult 1000 mL/hour, repeat if systolic BP &lt; 100</li>
<li>child only give on MO order</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Finger prick/blood ketones if available<sup>3</sup> — helps distinguish from <a href="/docs/Emergency/Diabetic-ketoacidosis">DKA</a></li>
<li>Contact MO urgently</li>
<li>Look for signs of acute illness<sup>1,3</sup> eg <a href="/docs/Emergency/Sepsisseptic-shock">Sepsis</a>, ACS, Stroke (common HHS precipitant)</li>
<li>Start fluid balance</li>
<li>Continuous cardiac monitoring</li>
<li>Do neurological observations - excessive fluids can cause cerebral oedema eg headache, ↓LOC, agitation/aggression</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3. Clinical assessment / 4. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>History, examination and ongoing management as for hyperglycaemic emergencies — see <a href="/docs/Emergency/Diabetic-ketoacidosis">DKA</a> (shared pages in source manual)</li>
<li>MO will arrange urgent evacuation + may order potassium replacement + IV short acting insulin infusion</li>
<li>Monitor closely until evacuated: hourly BGL, vital signs, neurological observations, strict fluid balance, continuous cardiac monitoring</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>As per MO</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">6. Referral/consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Patient need urgent referral to the main hospital</li>
</ul>
