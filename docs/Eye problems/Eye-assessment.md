<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Eye assessment – Adult/Child</h1>

<p>Understanding eye anatomy is crucial for public health nurses as it enables them to assess, identify, and address potential eye and vision problems contributing to overall patient well-being and preventing vision loss.</p>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with<sup>1</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Red, painful eye(s)</li>
<li>Changes to vision, photophobia, blurred vision</li>
<li>Discharge, tearing</li>
<li>Feeling like something in eye (Foreign body sensation)</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If history of substance in eye(s):
<ul>
<li>irrigate eye(s) with 1–2 L of sodium chloride 0.9% for ≥ 30 minutes:<sup>1</sup>
<ul>
<li>put in few drops of anaesthetic eyedrop (may use Lignocaine 1%) wait for 10-15 minutes then rinse</li>
<li>tap water/shower if nothing else available<sup>2,3</sup></li>
<li>go to <a href="/docs/Eye%20problems/Chemical-burn-to-eye">Chemical burn to eye</a>.</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If protruding foreign body (FB) - do not remove:<sup>4</sup>
<ul>
<li>consult MO urgently Ophthalmologist</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If severe pain, See MO Advice</li>
<li>If injury/trauma, ask about:
<ul>
<li>time and mechanism of injury eg sharp object, flash burn, exposure to laser</li>
<li>could there be a foreign body (FB):
<ul>
<li>ask about all activities in preceding 24 hours</li>
<li>especially activities that can cause high velocity projectiles eg hammering metal, angle grinding, operation of high speed machinery</li>
<li>possible contamination with plant material/soil</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>use of protective eyewear</li>
<li>if forceful blunt injury, suspect eyeball rupture ± ‘blow out’ fracture of the orbit</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If child always suspect injury as cause of red, painful eye:
<ul>
<li>were there any witnesses, is the injury consistent with explanation<sup>9</sup></li>
<li>if non-accidental injury is suspected, also see <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Child-protection">Child protection</a>.</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Ask about symptoms:
<ul>
<li>one or both eyes affected</li>
<li>rate of onset - sudden or over several hours<sup>6,7</sup></li>
<li>duration - is it getting better or worse</li>
<li>loss of vision, double or blurred vision<sup>6-8</sup></li>
<li>photophobia, pain or grittiness, redness, discharge<sup>6-8</sup></li>
<li>other eg flashing lights, floaters, headaches, halos around lights, fever</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Get past history:
<ul>
<li>diabetes, cold sores (herpes simplex virus), rheumatoid arthritis, ↑ BP</li>
<li>glasses, contact lenses (sleeping in, swimming in, prolonged use)<sup>6,8</sup></li>
<li>eye problems eg injury, cataracts, surgery</li>
<li>family history eg glaucoma</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Do vital signs
<ul>
<li>Examine eye</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="eye-examination">Eye examination</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If a red, swollen, tender eyelid + febrile assume <a href="/docs/Eye%20problems/Orbitalperiorbital-cellulitis">Orbital/periorbital cellulitis</a>.</li>
<li>Examine systematically</li>
<li>Measure VA</li>
<li>Do visual acuity (VA)
<ul>
<li>note: 3/3 or 6/6 VA does not exclude a serious condition. ↓VA may be long-standing or due to uncorrected refractive error</li>
<li>repeat VA using a multiple-pinhole occluder if available. If vision improves it likely means a VA issue that can be corrected with glasses, and less likely an acute issue</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Use ophthalmoscope set to +10D and held at 10 cm from eye or bright pen light<sup>5</sup></li>
<li>Check both eyes without putting pressure on the eye or forcing the lids open, including:<sup>4</sup>
<ul>
<li>eyelashes - any in-turned, crusted discharge</li>
<li>eyelids - do they fully close, any lacerations, swelling, redness, bruising</li>
<li>lower eyelid - colour of conjunctiva</li>
<li>sclera - bleeding, FB</li>
<li>cornea - clear or hazy, FB, white spot<sup>1</sup></li>
<li>anterior chamber - any blood (hyphaema) or pus (hypopyon)</li>
<li>pupils:
<ul>
<li>shape + reaction to light (do they react equally)</li>
<li>red reflex for any dimming, any cloudiness</li>
<li>any asymmetry in the position of and brightness of the corneal reflex</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>eye movements:
<ul>
<li>ask patient to look up, down, side to side - any pain</li>
<li>any restriction. If restricted upward movement consider orbital fracture in blunt injury</li>
<li>any double vision. To confirm if it is true double vision (and not just blur/ghosting), cover eye. If double vision disappears, it is not true double vision</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If eye swollen closed, gently lift lid to check for injuries + pupil size and reaction to light</li>
</ul>

<figure class="pccm-flowchart-figure">
<img src="/img/eye-problems/hyphaema.png" alt="Clinical photograph of hyphaema — blood pooling in the anterior chamber" />
<p class="pccm-flowchart-caption">Hyphaema</p>
</figure>

<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Always evert upper eyelid if red eye, sensation of FB, or grittiness:
<ul>
<li>check for/exclude retained FB</li>
<li>+ look for redness or small round bumps - may indicate conjunctivitis (bacterial, viral, allergic)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>IF SKILLED ONLY
<ul>
<li>Stain with fluorescein to help identify a FB or corneal defect eg abrasion</li>
<li>Measure intraocular pressure (IOP) if tonometer available and skilled</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="visual-acuity-va">Visual acuity (VA)</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Use Snellen or tumbling E chart positioned at the specified distance eg 3 or 6 metres, from the patient in a well lit area:<sup>10</sup>
<ul>
<li>test one eye at a time</li>
<li>ask patient to cover one eye with their palm and read or point in the direction of the E's, from the top of the chart left to right</li>
<li>record VA as 2 numbers. Top number is the distance from the chart, bottom number is the smallest line of letters/Es that can be read without mistakes eg 3/3, 6/9</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If cannot read the top letter at 3 or 6 metres:<sup>10</sup>
<ul>
<li>hold up your fingers at varying distances eg 5 metres, 4 metres etc and record vision as counting fingers (CF) (the maximum distance they can see between 5 and 1 metre eg VA CF 5 m)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If cannot see to count fingers, check if they can see hand movements or have perception of light by shining a torch into the eye at 10 cm<sup>5</sup></li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="fluorescein-examination-of-cornea">Fluorescein examination of cornea (eye drops or strips)</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Avoid if obvious or high likelihood of globe rupture or penetrating injury</li>
<li>If red eye(s) use separate strips/tissues for each eye<sup>1</sup></li>
<li>Fluorescein eye drops - use smallest amount possible, too much will flood the eye and a defect may be missed.<sup>4</sup> Gently dab the closed eye with a tissue to remove excess</li>
<li>Fluorescein strips - gently touch the strip to the inside of the lower eyelid, pre-moisten with saline if dry eye. Repeat if more dye is needed</li>
<li>Ask the patient to blink to spread the dye</li>
<li>Dye will pool or 'uptake' (stain) in damaged areas of cornea</li>
<li>In normal light uptake will look yellow. With blue light of ophthalmoscope uptake will be green:<sup>11</sup>
<ul>
<li>multiple pinpoint uptake - may be dry eye</li>
<li>sharp, well-defined border - may be abrasion(s)</li>
<li>round with fuzzy edges, branching (dendritic) pattern - may be herpes simplex virus</li>
<li>vertical lines on upper cornea - may be retained FB in eyelid</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="how-to-evert-an-eyelid">How to evert an eyelid<sup>1</sup></h2>

<figure class="pccm-flowchart-figure">

<p class="pccm-flowchart-caption">Image 1 — Ask patient to keep looking downwards as you take hold of the eyelashes and then gently pull the lid slightly towards you</p>
</figure>

<figure class="pccm-flowchart-figure">
<img src="/img/eye-problems/eyelid-eversion-2-render.png" alt="How to evert an eyelid — Image 2: cotton bud placed at lid crease with light downward pressure" />
<p class="pccm-flowchart-caption">Image 2 — Place cotton bud at the lid crease (or 5 mm from lid edge) and apply very light pressure downward with the bud</p>
</figure>

<figure class="pccm-flowchart-figure">
<img src="/img/eye-problems/eyelid-eversion-3-render.png" alt="How to evert an eyelid — Image 3: eyelid everted over cotton bud, bud removed" />
<p class="pccm-flowchart-caption">Image 3 — Evert the eyelid by using the eyelashes to gently pull the lid upwards over the bud. Remove the bud</p>
</figure>

<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Ask patient to keep looking downwards as you take hold of the eyelashes and then gently pull the lid slightly towards you (image 1)</li>
<li>Place cotton bud at the lid crease (or 5 mm from lid edge) and apply very light pressure downward with the bud (images 1 &amp; 2)</li>
<li>Evert the eyelid by using the eyelashes to gently pull the lid upwards over the bud. Remove the bud (image 3)</li>
</ul>
