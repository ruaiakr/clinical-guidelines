<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Hepatitis B – Adult/Child</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend<sup>1</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Acute hepatitis B is a notifiable condition and a vaccine-preventable disease</li>
<li>Screen all patients who test positive for an STI for hepatitis B virus (HBV)</li>
<li>Screening for HBV, HIV, and syphilis should be offered to all antenatal mothers during initial antenatal care (ANC) clinic</li>
<li>Use WHO pre-qualified point-of-care rapid diagnostic tests for screening, e.g., Determine™ HBsAg2 test</li>
<li>HBV-positive individuals must be referred to local clinics and linked to hepatitis care services</li>
<li>All neonates must be given timely HBV birth dose vaccination within 24 hours of birth</li>
<li>Advise not to share razors, toothbrushes, unsafe tattooing tools or any blood-contaminated personal items</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background<sup>1,2</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Hepatitis B is endemic in Kiribati</li>
<li>Acute HBV infection is often asymptomatic:
<ul>
<li>50–70% of adults and up to 90% of children have subclinical infection</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Risk of chronic infection decreases with age:
<ul>
<li>~90% of infants infected at birth develop chronic HBV</li>
<li>&lt;10% of adults do</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Up to 25% of people with chronic HBV may develop cirrhosis, liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma</li>
<li>Transmission routes:
<ul>
<li>Perinatal (mother-to-child) during child-birth or obstetric interventions</li>
<li>Percutaneous (e.g. injecting drug use)</li>
<li>Sexual contact</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Patients with acute HBV infection require follow-up HbsAg testing after 6 months</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with<sup>1,3</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>No symptoms</li>
<li>If present, general symptoms are:
<ul>
<li>Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain</li>
<li>Loss of appetite</li>
<li>Rash, low-grade fever</li>
<li>Jaundice (yellow skin/eyes), dark urine, pale stools</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Laboratory findings:
<ul>
<li>HBsAg positive (in a person previously negative)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>HBV is a notifiable disease and should be reported to the National Hepatitis Program</li>
<li>Acute infection is usually self-limiting and can be managed as an out-patient case</li>
<li>Counselling and education of patient and immediate family of condition, transmission, treatment options and prevention strategies</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">3. Clinical assessment<sup>2,3</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>History:
<ul>
<li>Mode of exposure: unsafe sex, injecting drugs, shared equipment, tattoos</li>
<li>Family history of liver disease or HBV</li>
<li>Vaccination history</li>
<li>In pregnant patients: gestation, antenatal care attendance</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Examination:
<ul>
<li>Vitals (Q-ADDS/CEWT)</li>
<li>Check for liver tenderness, hepatomegaly, ascites, or history of haematemesis/melena</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Investigations:
<ul>
<li>HBsAg</li>
<li>+/- Hepatitis A, C, D serology (discuss with Hepatitis Program)</li>
<li>LFTs, INR – calculate APRI-score</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>In antenatal patients:
<ul>
<li>Initiate antiviral prophylaxis at 24 weeks (or earlier if advised) for all HBsAg-positive mothers</li>
<li>Outer island mothers should begin treatment early and remain on therapy post-delivery</li>
<li>Continue antiviral treatment until at least 6 weeks postpartum</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="management">4. Management<sup>3</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Consult Medical Officer (MO) for all cases where hepatitis B is suspected or jaundice is detected</li>
<li>Urgent referral if INR ≥ 1.5 or signs of liver failure</li>
<li>Treatment is supportive:
<ul>
<li>Bed rest if symptomatic</li>
<li>Avoid alcohol and paracetamol</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If HbsAg is positive:
<ul>
<li>Test for HBeAg and consider HBV DNA (if available)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If anti-HBs negative, consider vaccination</li>
<li>Educate patient and household:
<ul>
<li>Modes of transmission, safe practices, importance of hygiene</li>
<li>Do not share personal items</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Perform contact tracing (exposure window: up to 180 days prior)</li>
<li>Offer HBV vaccine to unvaccinated contacts – review vaccination records for children and offer catch-up doses</li>
<li>For pregnant women:
<ul>
<li>Link to hepatitis care services (clinic)</li>
<li>Prioritize timely (&lt;24hrs) provision of hepatitis B birth dose vaccination to newborns of HBV-positive mothers – including home births</li>
<li>Monitor treatment continuation postnatally</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="follow-up">5. Follow up<sup>1</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Review within 24 hours</li>
<li>Reinforce adherence, education, contact tracing, and preventative strategies (e.g., childhood vaccinations)</li>
<li>Ensure ongoing antenatal follow-up if patient is pregnant</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="referral-consultation">6. Referral / consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Consult MO as required</li>
<li>Notify Public Health Unit / National Hepatitis Program</li>
<li>Refer to specialist/hepatitis clinic at Tungaru Central Hospital (especially for chronic HBV or liver failure)</li>
<li>For antenatal patients, ensure coordination between MCH and clinical services for PMTCT compliance</li>
</ul>

</div>
