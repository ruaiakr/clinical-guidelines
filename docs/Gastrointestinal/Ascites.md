<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Ascites – Adult</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<p>Ascites is the accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity and is the most common complication of liver cirrhosis, regardless of the cause (e.g. hepatitis B, alcohol). Ascites is primarily caused by portal hypertension with contributing circulatory, vascular, functional, and biochemical abnormalities that lead to fluid retention. It is typically managed through dietary sodium restriction and the use of diuretics.</p>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p>The primary goals of therapy are to:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Minimise ascitic fluid volume</li>
<li>Decrease peripheral oedema</li>
<li>Improve quality of life (e.g. improve appetite, ease of movement, reduce discomfort and shortness of breath)</li>
<li>Complications of ascites include <a href="/docs/Gastrointestinal/Spontaneous-Bacterial-Peritonitis">spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)</a> and hepatorenal syndrome. Early diagnosis and effective volume control can prevent complications and reduce the need for hospitalisation.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Abdominal distension</li>
<li>Fullness in the flanks</li>
<li>Weight gain</li>
<li>Shortness of breath</li>
<li>Reduced appetite or early satiety</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>On physical examination:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Distended abdomen</li>
<li>Shifting dullness (suggests &gt;500 mL fluid)</li>
<li>Fluid thrill (suggests &gt;1 L fluid)</li>
<li>Signs of chronic liver disease (e.g. jaundice, spider naevi, palmar erythema)</li>
<li>+/- Ultrasound scan (if available) may be used to confirm diagnosis or monitor treatment response</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Call for help – inform MO urgently</li>
<li>Triage patient and assess vital signs</li>
<li>If available and trained, consider abdominal paracentesis (therapeutic and/or diagnostic)</li>
<li>Discontinue alcohol use</li>
<li>Discontinue medications that may reduce renal perfusion – e.g. NSAIDs, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors (discuss with MO)</li>
<li>Treat underlying liver disease – if HBsAg-positive, initiate Tenofovir and link patient to Hepatitis Program</li>
<li>Begin dietary education with sodium restriction &lt;2g/day</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Confirm ascites through physical signs and/or ultrasound if available</li>
<li>Evaluate for signs of liver cirrhosis and complications</li>
<li>Assess for signs of SBP (fever, abdominal tenderness, altered mental status)</li>
<li>Assess nutritional status, mobility, and overall wellbeing</li>
<li>Consider other comorbidities or medications contributing to fluid retention</li>
<li>Monitor daily weight for baseline</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="management">3. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Dietary management:
<ul>
<li>Sodium restriction to &lt;2g/day (low-salt diet)</li>
<li>Encourage adequate nutrition (small frequent meals)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Diuretic therapy:</li>
<li>Start with:
<ul>
<li>Spironolactone 100 mg PO OD</li>
<li>Furosemide 40 mg PO OD</li>
<li>For small patients, consider starting at half doses:
<ul>
<li>Spironolactone 50 mg PO OD</li>
<li>Furosemide 20 mg PO OD</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Titration:
<ul>
<li>Titrate doses upwards only after ≥3–5 days</li>
<li>Maintain a 100:40 mg spironolactone:furosemide ratio</li>
<li>Maximum doses:
<ul>
<li>Spironolactone 400 mg/day</li>
<li>Furosemide 160 mg/day</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Monitoring:
<ul>
<li>Aim for daily weight loss of 500–1000 g/day</li>
<li>Monitor blood pressure, urine output, and signs of dehydration or electrolyte disturbance</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Monitor for muscle cramps, dizziness, or confusion</li>
<li>Adjust doses in consultation with MO</li>
<li>Monitor for signs of complications such as SBP or renal dysfunction</li>
<li>If patient is not improving, refer to tertiary hospital for further care (e.g. liver function tests, electrolytes, endoscopy)</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="follow-up">4. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Monitor weight daily where possible</li>
<li>Review diuretic response after 3–5 days</li>
<li>Continue dietary counselling and monitor adherence to sodium restriction</li>
<li>Educate patient and family on signs of complications (e.g. fever, confusion, abdominal pain)</li>
<li>Ensure linkage to Hepatitis Program if HBsAg positive</li>
<li>Consider referral for ongoing</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="referral-consultation">5. Referral / consultation</h2>
<p>Refer to medical officer or hospital if:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Ascites is severe or refractory to diuretics</li>
<li>Symptoms of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis are present</li>
<li>Signs of renal impairment or electrolyte imbalance</li>
<li>Paracentesis is required but cannot be safely performed locally</li>
<li>Specialist review needed for liver disease or complications (e.g. hepatology, endoscopy)</li>
</ul>
