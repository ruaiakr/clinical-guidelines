Maneaba: I-Kiribati Nursing Model of Care
Maneaba means accommodating and welcoming: A safe place to rest, to
meet or get together or take refuge.
Historically, the maneaba was considered a meeting place for villages/
island to congregate and make important decisions. Customarily, subject
to context e.g. church gatherings/tribal affiliations or individual status,
special seating allocations within the maneaba are adhered to accordingly.
Traditional beliefs suggests that the Maneaba had been ‘purposefully’ built
with a lowly-positioned roof to serve as a constant reminder to I-Kiribati
that on entering the dwelling, they must always bow or kneel to show
respect for their Unimwane & Unaine / Elders. This was also symbolic of
acknowledging their ancestral gods, prior to the arrival of Christianity on
their shores.
In present-day, although many members of the Kiribati community have
adapted to Western ideology, traditional values and beliefs are still
maintained. Hence, the symbolic gesture in the use of the Maneaba as a
cultural framework for nursing is befitting.
This is likened to other Polynesian conceptual frameworks where
traditional thatched or flax-woven shelters have been utilised
metaphorically to depict the significance of culture in their care delivery.

Kiribati Decision-Making Framework
(Abridged Version)
The Kiribati Decision-Making Framework (KDMF) provides a clear and
consistent process for nurses and medical assistants to determine whether
a clinical activity should be undertaken. This summary outlines the key
principles only. All clinicians must refer to the full KDMF for detailed
requirements, examples, and procedural steps.
Purpose
Clinical decision-making is a core professional responsibility. In Kiribati,
where many clinicians work with limited resources or in remote outer
island settings, decisions about treatment, medication administration,
delegation, and escalation must be safe, lawful, and accountable. The
KDMF supports clinicians by providing a structured approach to these
decisions, ensuring consistent and patient-centred care across all facilities.
The Framework is grounded in the ICN Definition of ‘Nursing’ and of ‘a
Nurse’ (2026).
Why the Framework Is Needed
Kiribati’s health system requires a structured decision support process
because of:
•	 Geographical isolation and limited access to higher-level services
•	 Resource constraints that affect what can be done safely in each setting
•	 Multiple cadres working together across facilities and islands
•	 Legal requirements governing the use of medicines
•	 Ongoing system development, including new PCCM interventions
The KDMF ensures that decisions are consistent, safe, and legally
defensible in all contexts.

Foundations of Safe Decision-Making
The KDMF is based on three domains that must all be met before any
activity is undertaken:
1.	 Regulation and Legislation: activities must comply with the Legislation
(ie: Medicines Act 2018), MHMS policy, and professional regulation and
standards.
2.	 Professional Competence: clinicians must be ecducated / trained,
competent, and have recency of practice.
3.	 Context of Practice: decisions must reflect local resources, supervision,
staffing, and referral pathways.
Only when all three domains are satisfied is an activity considered within
safe scope of practice.
Guiding Principles
The Framework is underpinned by the following principles:
•	 Patient safety comes first in all decisions.
•	 Practice must be lawful and within authorised scope.
•	 Accountability is individual—each clinician is responsible for their own
decisions.
•	 Competence and recency are essential for safe practice.
•	 Context determines safety, particularly in remote or resource-limited
settings.
•	 Collaboration with colleagues strengthens decision-making.
•	 Alignment with professional identity, consistent with the ICN Definitions.

Supervision and Delegation
Supervision and delegation are integral to scope of practice in Kiribati.
Registered nurses remain accountable for decisions to delegate and must
ensure the delegatee is authorised, competent, and supported. Supervision
may be direct, indirect, or remote, depending on the complexity of the
activity and the competence of the clinician involved. These processes
support patient safety and help build workforce capacity.
Expanding Scope of Practice
Scope of practice is dynamic. Expansion must occur through a structured
and governed process, including legal review, MHMS endorsement,
education and training, supervised practice, competence assessment, and
ongoing monitoring. Any new activity added to scope must remain lawful,
supported, and in the best interests of patients.
This summary is for quick reference only. All nurses and medical assistants
must refer to the full Kiribati Decision-Making Framework for complete
guidance, detailed explanations, and examples.
Statutory Authority to Use Medicines
Medicines Act 2018, Part 4
This manual must be read together with the Kiribati Medicines Act 2018
(Part 4, ss.17–20). The manual does not create new authorities; it guides
practice within the authorities below and within each practitioner’s scope,
competence and local governance.

Who may prescribe:
•	 Medical practitioners may prescribe any scheduled medicine for persons
under their care.
•	 Dentists may prescribe Level D medicines for persons under their care.
•	 No other cadre has statutory prescribing authority under the Act
(veterinary prescribers are limited to Level V for animals).
Who may supply / dispense / administer:
•	 Pharmacists may obtain, compound, prepare, store, distribute, supply or
dispense any medicine on the Register of Medicines.
•	 Medical practitioners may administer any medicine on the Register for
persons under their care.
•	 Nurses may dispense and administer Level 1 and Level 2 medicines for
persons under their care.
•	 Medical assistants, and nurses supervised by a medical assistant, may
dispense and administer Level 1, Level 2 and Level 2B medicines for
persons under their care.
Using PCCM protocols
•	 Protocols list recommended medicines and doses. You may only carry out
the parts of a protocol that fall within your statutory authority and scope.
•	 Where a protocol includes a medicine above your authorised level (e.g.,
Level 2B when you are a nurse not under medical-assistant supervision,
or any medicine requiring a prescription), a medical practitioner (or
dentist for Level D) must prescribe and, where applicable, administer.
•	 All actions must be for particular persons under your care, documented,
and undertaken with required supervision, competence and local
approvals.
Medicine schedules noted in protocols
•	 Schedules referenced in protocols (Level 1, Level 2, Level 2B, Level D,
Level V) must be applied as per ss.18–19 and the Register of Medicines.
•	 The Pharmacy team will provide the authoritative schedule status for
each medicine; the manual will reflect these once confirmed. Until then,
check the Register of Medicines or consult MHMS Pharmacy before
proceeding.
