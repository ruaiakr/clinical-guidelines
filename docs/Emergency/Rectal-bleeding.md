<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Rectal bleeding (Anorectal disease)</h1>
<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p>Rectal bleeding may be minor spotting or significant blood loss. It may appear as blood mixed with stool, blood coating the stool, or blood leaking from the rectum.</p>
<p><strong>The cause may be benign or serious, so a careful history and examination are</strong></p>
<p>essential.</p>
<p><strong>Common benign causes include:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Haemorrhoids</li>
<li>Anal fissure</li>
<li>Anorectal abscesses (including pilonidal disease)</li>
<li>Rectal prolapse Other causes and important differentials include:</li>
<li>Sexually transmitted infections</li>
<li>Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)</li>
<li>Malignancy (colorectal or anal cancer)</li>
<li>Proctalgia fugax</li>
<li>Proctitis</li>
<li>Anorectal foreign bodies</li>
<li>Local trauma (including sexual trauma)</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommendations</h2>
<p>Take a detailed history and examine the perianal region carefully. Rectal bleeding should never be assumed to be benign without excluding serious pathology. Refer if signs of systemic illness, large volume bleeding, or failure to respond to conservative management.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Bright red blood mixed with stool</li>
<li>Blood coating stool or leaking from the anus</li>
<li>Perianal pain, swelling or itching</li>
<li>Difficulty passing stool</li>
<li>Fever, anorexia (if abscess)</li>
<li>Recent weight loss, change in bowel habit</li>
<li>Mucous discharge or tenesmus (urge to pass stool)</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2.	 Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Assess haemodynamic stability (pulse, BP)</li>
<li>If heavy bleeding or signs of anaemia, lie patient flat and monitor closely</li>
<li>Provide analgesia as needed</li>
<li>Begin IV fluids if unstable or signs of dehydration</li>
<li>Do not delay rectal examination if serious pathology suspected</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3.	 Clinical assessment</h2>
<p><strong>Detailed history should include:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Onset, duration, and amount of bleeding</li>
<li>Colour of blood – bright red vs dark or maroon</li>
<li>Relation of blood to stool (mixed in, coating, separate)</li>
<li>Straining or hard stools</li>
<li>Past history of haemorrhoids</li>
<li>Change in bowel habits – constipation, diarrhoea, mucous</li>
<li>Associated symptoms: rectal pain, itch, abdominal pain, weight loss</li>
<li>History of IBD, cancer, recent polypectomy</li>
<li>History of rectal or sexual trauma</li>
<li>Use of aspirin, NSAIDs, anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents Examination:</li>
<li>Inspect perianal area for:</li>
<li>External haemorrhoids</li>
<li>Skin tags</li>
<li>Anal fissures</li>
<li>Haematomas</li>
<li>Perform rectal examination where safe and appropriate to check for masses, tenderness, or stool colour</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5.	 Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Reassess after 5–7 days or earlier if worsening</li>
<li>Monitor for recurrence or signs of infection</li>
<li>If bleeding persists or is associated with systemic symptoms, refer for specialist assessment</li>
<li>Encourage lifestyle changes to reduce straining and constipation</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">6.	 Referral / consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Refer to doctor if:<ul><li>Bleeding is persistent, recurrent, or unexplained</li><li>Suspicion of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease</li><li>Large perianal abscess requiring incision and drainage</li><li>Anal fissure not healing or very painful</li></ul></li>
<li>Withhold anticoagulants or antiplatelets temporarily if appropriate—consult doctor first</li>
</ul>
