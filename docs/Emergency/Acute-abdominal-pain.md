<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Acute abdominal pain</h1>
<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p><strong>Acute abdomen refers to a sudden onset of severe abdominal pain that may</strong></p>
<p>require urgent medical attention. The cause can be gastrointestinal, urological, reproductive, respiratory, or related to trauma. A diagram classifying causes of acute abdominal pain should be inserted here.</p>
<p><strong>In females of reproductive age, always consider:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Ectopic pregnancy – particularly if pain is accompanied by vaginal bleeding</li>
<li>Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) – particularly if &lt;30 years and sexually active, with new pelvic pain In males:</li>
<li>Testicular torsion must be considered in all males with abdominal pain</li>
<li>In male infants with inconsolable crying, examine for testicular or scrotal pathology A definitive diagnosis is not always required immediately. However, a detailed and structured history and examination are critical to guide next steps and identify red flags.</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommendations</h2>
<p>Always take a comprehensive pain history and perform a full abdominal and genital examination. Urgently consult or refer patients with red flags such as signs of peritonitis, sepsis, shock, or suspected ectopic pregnancy or testicular torsion.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Sudden or gradual onset of abdominal pain</li>
<li>Pain may be continuous or intermittent</li>
<li>Pain may be localised or generalised</li>
<li>Pain may radiate to the back or shoulder tip</li>
<li>Associated symptoms may include:<ul><li>Nausea or vomiting (bile, blood, or faecal-smelling)</li><li>Diarrhoea or constipation</li><li>Fever or shortness of breath</li><li>Dysuria, haematuria</li><li>Vaginal bleeding or discharge</li><li>Poor feeding, refusal to eat (children)</li><li>Recent trauma to abdomen, scrotum, or genital area</li><li>Weight loss or anorexia</li></ul></li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2.	 Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Apply DRSABCD approach</li>
<li>If unstable: start IV access and fluids</li>
<li>Keep nil by mouth</li>
<li>Provide pain relief as needed</li>
<li>If testicular torsion or ectopic pregnancy suspected: urgent referral</li>
<li>Monitor vital signs</li>
<li>Do not delay consultation in unstable patients or those with red flags</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3.	 Clinical assessment</h2>
<p><strong>History of pain</strong></p>
<p><strong>Ask about:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Site – ask patient to point to location</li>
<li>Onset – sudden, gradual; what were they doing when it started</li>
<li>Character – sharp, dull, stabbing, crampy, burning</li>
<li>Radiation – e.g. to back, shoulder tip</li>
<li>Timing – first onset, duration, prior episodes</li>
<li>Exacerbating/alleviating factors – worse with movement? relieved by lying still?</li>
<li>Severity – scale 1–10, impact on sleep or daily activity Associated symptoms Ask about:</li>
<li>Vomiting – bile, blood, green, faecal odour</li>
<li>Bowel habits – diarrhoea, constipation, black or bloody stools, last motion</li>
<li>Urine – frequency, burning, urgency, blood</li>
<li>Vaginal – bleeding, discharge</li>
<li>Respiratory symptoms – shortness of breath, cough (to consider pneumonia)</li>
<li>Weight loss, poor feeding, food refusal</li>
<li>Foreign body ingestion or recent trauma (especially in children) Physical examination Perform a full abdominal examination:</li>
<li>Look:<ul><li>Shape and symmetry (distended, sunken)</li><li>Surgical scars, bruises, hernias, distended veins</li><li>Signs of non-accidental injury</li></ul></li>
<li>Palpate:<ul><li>Start away from site of pain; light then deep palpation in 9 areas</li><li>Assess for rebound tenderness (pain when pressure is released)</li><li>Localised tenderness, distension, guarding (involuntary tightening)</li><li>Pain out of proportion to findings</li><li>Palpate kidneys with one hand under flank and one above</li></ul></li>
<li>Auscultate:<ul><li>Listen for bowel sounds in all quadrants</li><li>Normal: low-pitched, gurgling 2–5/min</li><li>Hyperactive or high-pitched: consider obstruction</li><li>Absent: consider ileus or peritonitis</li></ul></li>
<li>Percuss:<ul><li>Tap all four quadrants to assess for dullness or tympany</li></ul></li>
<li>Chest examination:<ul><li>Listen for crackles or wheeze, as lower lobe pneumonia can cause abdominal pain</li></ul></li>
<li>In males:<ul><li>Examine testes for torsion</li></ul></li>
<li>In females:<ul><li>Consider pelvic examination or pregnancy test if reproductive age</li></ul></li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5.	 Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Reassess regularly, especially if diagnosis unclear</li>
<li>Monitor vital signs and symptoms closely</li>
<li>Repeat abdominal examination if symptoms persist or worsen</li>
<li>If diagnosis changes, adjust management plan and refer if needed</li>
</ul>
