# Vaginal itchiness (Kiribati Original Protocol)

Vaginal itchiness (Kiribati Original Protocol)
Recommend
•	 Vaginal itchiness is a common complaint and may be caused by infectious,
irritant, or allergic conditions. Accurate diagnosis is important to ensure
appropriate treatment and avoid unnecessary antibiotics.
•	 If suspicious cervical or vulval lesions are seen, refer for visual inspection with
acetic acid (VIA), colposcopy, or treatment such as LLETZ where appropriate.
Background
•	 Vaginal itchiness may be caused by infections such as candidiasis, bacterial
vaginosis (BV), or trichomoniasis, as well as irritant contact dermatitis, atrophic
vaginitis (in post-menopausal women), or rarely cervical or vulval intraepithelial
neoplasia. Diagnosis is largely clinical in low-resource settings, supported by pH
testing and microscopy where available.
1.
May present with
•	 Vaginal or vulval itching, burning, irritation
•	 Vaginal discharge (may be thin, thick, white, grey, green, or yellow)
•	 Odour (especially fishy, suggestive of BV)
•	 Dysuria or superficial dyspareunia
•	 Redness, swelling, or visible lesions on vulva or cervix
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Assess and reassure
•	 Provide a private space for examination
•	 Always consider and exclude pregnancy if relevant
•	 Examine vulva and vagina with clean gloves and light source
•	 Look for:

–
Discharge characteristics (colour, consistency, odour)

–
Vulval or vaginal redness or swelling

–
Ulcers, sores, or white plaques

–
Cervical appearance (contact bleeding, lesions, ectopy)

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Ask about:

–
Onset, duration, and severity of itching

–
Changes in discharge or odour

–
Sexual history and contraception

–
Recent antibiotic use or new products (e.g. soaps, sprays)

–
Menstrual status or menopause
•	 Consider vaginal pH testing if available:

–
pH >4.5 suggests BV or trichomoniasis

–
pH ≤4.5 suggests candidiasis

4.	 Management
Empiric treatment based on clinical suspicion if diagnostic testing not available.
•	 Suspected Candidiasis (no odour, thick white discharge, intense itching):

–
Clotrimazole pessary 500 mg single dose at night
OR

–
Miconazole 1200 mg single dose vaginal suppository
OR

–
Fluconazole 150 mg oral single dose (if available)
•	 Suspected Bacterial Vaginosis (fishy odour, greyish discharge, mild irritation):

–
Metronidazole 400 mg orally twice daily for 7 days
OR

–
Metronidazole 2 g orally as a single dose (less preferred if recurrent)
•	 Suspected Trichomoniasis (frothy discharge, STI risk):

–
Metronidazole 2 g orally single dose

–
Treat sexual partner(s) as well

–
Advise to abstain from sex until both partners treated
•	 Irritant dermatitis:

–
Avoid irritants (soap, douches, sprays)

–
Apply hydrocortisone cream 1% thinly to affected area twice daily for up to 7
days
•	 Atrophic vaginitis (postmenopausal women):

–
Consider topical oestrogen pessary (e.g. estriol 0.5 mg vaginal pessary at
night for 2–4 weeks then twice weekly) if recurrent or persistent symptoms

–
Discuss with MO before prescribing
•	 If unclear diagnosis:

–
Consider combined treatment:

–
Metronidazole 400 mg bd x 7 days

–
Clotrimazole 500 mg pessary single dose

–
Review response in 7 days
5.	 Follow up
•	 Review in 7 days if symptoms persist
•	 Reinforce STI prevention and safe sex practices
•	 Advise not to douche or use irritants in the genital area
•	 Counsel on recurrence prevention for candidiasis (avoid tight clothing, stay dry)
