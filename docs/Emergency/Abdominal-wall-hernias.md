# Abdominal wall hernias

Abdominal wall hernias
Background
A hernia is defined as the protrusion of a viscus or its contents through a weakness
in the abdominal wall. These are a common cause of abdominal pain and must be
assessed during abdominal examination.
Hernias can be classified as follows:
1.
Anatomical type:

–
Inguinal hernia

–
Umbilical hernia
2.
Reducibility:

–
Reducible hernia

–
Non-reducible (incarcerated or strangulated) hernia
Hernias may present without symptoms or may become painful or obstructed.
Bowel herniation into the hernia sac may lead to obstruction, strangulation, or
ischaemia.
Recommendations
All patients presenting with abdominal pain or swelling should be examined
for hernia. Refer urgently if the hernia is irreducible, painful, or associated with
symptoms of bowel obstruction. Avoid attempts to manually reduce the hernia.
1.
May present with
•	 Abnormal swelling, especially in the umbilical or groin area
•	 Swelling may reduce when lying flat (if reducible)
•	 Pain or tenderness over the swelling
•	 Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal distension if bowel obstruction present
•	 Incarcerated or strangulated hernias may present with:

–
Severe abdominal pain

–
Non-reducible tender mass

–
Constipation or no passage of flatus

–
Fever or signs of sepsis

2.	 Immediate management
If signs of intestinal involvement are present (severe abdominal pain, vomiting,
distension):
•	 Admit the patient immediately
•	 Keep nil by mouth (NBM)
•	 Insert nasogastric tube for decompression and free drainage
•	 Start IV fluid resuscitation
•	 Start broad-spectrum antibiotics
•	 Provide analgesia
•	 Arrange urgent referral to the main hospital
•	 Surgical consultation must occur within 6 hours to avoid strangulation and
ischaemic bowel

If no signs of intestinal involvement:
•	 Provide pain relief
•	 Advise rest and avoid lifting or strenuous activity
•	 Avoid massage or manual reduction (may risk ischaemia)
•	 Refer to surgeon for elective hernia repair
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Inspect for visible abdominal or groin swelling
•	 Palpate swelling: assess for tenderness and reducibility
•	 Ask the patient to cough or stand to assess impulse
•	 Check for signs of bowel obstruction:

–
Distension

–
Bowel sounds (high-pitched or absent)

–
Vomiting or failure to pass stool/gas

•	 Educate patient to return immediately if pain increases or hernia becomes

5.	 Follow up
•	 Monitor for pain, recurrence, or worsening signs
•	 Reassess hernia regularly for changes in size, tenderness, or reducibility
•	 For reducible hernias, refer for elective surgical repair
•	 For non-reducible or symptomatic hernias, urgent referral is required

6.	 Referral / consultation
•	 Urgent referral to surgical team if:

–
Painful, tender, or non-reducible hernia

–
Signs of bowel obstruction or sepsis
•	 Elective referral for reducible hernias without complications
•	 Do not attempt manual reduction of incarcerated or strangulated hernias
outside hospital setting
