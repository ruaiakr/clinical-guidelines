Rectal bleeding (Anorectal disease)
Background
Rectal bleeding may be minor spotting or significant blood loss. It may appear as
blood mixed with stool, blood coating the stool, or blood leaking from the rectum.
The cause may be benign or serious, so a careful history and examination are
essential.
Common benign causes include:
•	 Haemorrhoids
•	 Anal fissure
•	 Anorectal abscesses (including pilonidal disease)
•	 Rectal prolapse
Other causes and important differentials include:
•	 Sexually transmitted infections
•	 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
•	 Malignancy (colorectal or anal cancer)
•	 Proctalgia fugax
•	 Proctitis
•	 Anorectal foreign bodies
•	 Local trauma (including sexual trauma)
Recommendations
Take a detailed history and examine the perianal region carefully. Rectal bleeding
should never be assumed to be benign without excluding serious pathology. Refer if
signs of systemic illness, large volume bleeding, or failure to respond to conservative
management.
1.
May present with
•	 Bright red blood mixed with stool
•	 Blood coating stool or leaking from the anus
•	 Perianal pain, swelling or itching
•	 Difficulty passing stool
•	 Fever, anorexia (if abscess)
•	 Recent weight loss, change in bowel habit
•	 Mucous discharge or tenesmus (urge to pass stool)

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Assess haemodynamic stability (pulse, BP)
•	 If heavy bleeding or signs of anaemia, lie
patient flat and monitor closely
•	 Provide analgesia as needed
•	 Begin IV fluids if unstable or signs of
dehydration
•	 Do not delay rectal examination if serious
pathology suspected
3.	 Clinical assessment
Detailed history should include:
•	 Onset, duration, and amount of bleeding
•	 Colour of blood – bright red vs dark or maroon
•	 Relation of blood to stool (mixed in, coating, separate)
•	 Straining or hard stools
•	 Past history of haemorrhoids
•	 Change in bowel habits – constipation, diarrhoea, mucous
•	 Associated symptoms: rectal pain, itch, abdominal pain, weight loss
•	 History of IBD, cancer, recent polypectomy
•	 History of rectal or sexual trauma
•	 Use of aspirin, NSAIDs, anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents
Examination:
•	 Inspect perianal area for:
•	 External haemorrhoids
•	 Skin tags
•	 Anal fissures
•	 Haematomas
•	 Perform rectal examination where safe
and appropriate to check for masses,
tenderness, or stool colour

4.	 Management
If haemorrhoids:
•	 Advise patient to:
•	 Avoid straining
•	 Maintain fibre-rich diet and good hydration
•	 Respond to urge to defecate—don’t delay bowel motion
•	 Avoid attempting to force bowel movement without urge
