# Cholecystitis

Cholecystitis
Background
Cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder, most commonly caused by
gallstones. Gallstone disease is one of the most frequent abdominal conditions
requiring hospital admission in the developed world. In the UK alone, 5.5
million people are estimated to have gallstones, and approximately 50,000
cholecystectomies are performed annually.
Gallstones are found 2–3 times more frequently in women than in Obesity is the
strongest independent risk factor for gallstone development in women. This is likely
due to the effect of oestrogen, which increases the movement of cholesterol from
the bloodstream into the liver, contributing to gallstone formation.
Recommendations
If acute cholecystitis is suspected, admit the patient immediately. Resuscitate if
there are signs of sepsis or dehydration. Keep the patient nil by mouth (NBM),
initiate IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics, and provide opiate analgesia. Refer
to the main hospital or surgical team, especially if pain persists or the patient is
unstable.
1.
May present with
•	 Constant right upper quadrant (RUQ) abdominal pain (not colicky)
•	 Pain lasting 2–6 hours, may persist up to 24 hours
•	 Nausea and vomiting – may occur but are not dominant features
•	 Murphy’s sign positive (pain on inspiration during RUQ palpation)
•	 Chronic cholecystitis may present with vague symptoms such as:

–
Intolerance of fatty foods

–
Early satiety

–
Recurrent similar episodes
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Admit patient
•	 Apply DRSABCD and initiate resuscitation if signs of dehydration or sepsis
•	 Insert two large bore IV cannulae
•	 Insert urinary catheter (IDC) and measure urine output if unstable
•	 Keep nil by mouth (NBM)
•	 Start IV fluids and broad-spectrum antibiotics
•	 Provide opiate analgesia
•	 Urgently refer to the surgical team or main hospital if instability or pain persists

–

•	 Look for signs of systemic sepsis (tachycardia, hypotension, low urine output)
•	 Assess hydration and urine output

5.	 Follow up
•	 Monitor temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and urine output
•	 Reassess pain control and clinical response to antibiotics
•	 If pain resolves and sepsis is controlled, continue supportive care and plan for
surgical referral
•	 If pain or signs of instability persist, escalate care
6.	 Referral / consultation
•	 Consult surgeon for all patients with suspected acute cholecystitis
•	 Urgent referral to senior surgeon and/or Critical Care if instability persists despite
resuscitation
•	 Refer to the main hospital if surgical care or imaging is not available on site
