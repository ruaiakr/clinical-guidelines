<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Scarlet Fever</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Early diagnosis and antibiotic treatment are key to preventing complications like rheumatic fever.</li>
<li>Ensure the patient stays hydrated and receives adequate rest.</li>
<li>Isolate the patient to reduce transmission to others, particularly in close-contact settings like schools.</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Scarlet fever is a bacterial infection caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, which is the same bacterium that causes strep throat. It primarily affects children and is characterized by a red, sandpaper-like rash and high fever. While it can be treated with antibiotics, it requires prompt intervention to prevent complications.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>High fever (above 38.3°C)</li>
<li>Red, sore throat with white patches. See <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Sore-throat">Sore throat</a> / <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Pharyngitis-tonsillitis">Pharyngitis/tonsillitis</a>.</li>
<li>A red rash that feels like sandpaper (often starting on the neck, chest, and back)</li>
<li>"Strawberry tongue" (bright red, swollen tongue with prominent papillae)</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Initiate oral antibiotics (e.g., penicillin) as soon as the diagnosis is confirmed.</li>
<li>Offer antipyretics (e.g., paracetamol) to manage fever.</li>
<li>Ensure proper hydration and nutrition.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Examine the throat for redness and pus and the rash for characteristic features.</li>
<li>Assess for any signs of complications such as septic arthritis or glomerulonephritis. See <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Acutely-swollenpainful-joint">Acutely swollen/painful joint</a> and <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Acute-post-streptococcal-glomerulonephritis">APSGN</a>.</li>
<li>Review the patient's immunization and family history to check for other possible sources of infection.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="management">4. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Initiate antibiotic therapy promptly and monitor for improvement.</li>
<li>Provide symptomatic care including hydration and pain management.</li>
<li>Educate the patient and caregivers about preventing the spread of infection.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="follow-up">5. Follow-up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Follow-up after 2-3 days to ensure the patient is responding to antibiotics.</li>
<li>Reassess if symptoms persist or worsen, as this may indicate complications.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="referral-consultation">6. Referral/Consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Refer to a specialist if complications such as <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Acute-rheumatic-fever">rheumatic fever</a>, glomerulonephritis, or septic arthritis are suspected.</li>
</ul>
