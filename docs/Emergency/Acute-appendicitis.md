<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Acute appendicitis</h1>
<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p><strong>Acute appendicitis is the most common abdominal surgical emergency in the</strong></p>
<p>world, with a lifetime risk of 8.6% in males and 6.9% in females. It is caused by inflammation of the appendix and requires prompt recognition and referral. The classical history involves colicky central abdominal pain that localises to the right iliac fossa (RIF) within 6–12 hours and becomes constant. However, this pattern is only present in approximately half of patients, and clinical judgement must be used to support diagnosis.</p>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommendations</h2>
<p>All suspected cases of acute appendicitis should be admitted immediately. Keep nil by mouth (NBM), start triple IV antibiotics (Gentamicin, Ampicillin and Metronidazole), hydrate with IV fluids, provide analgesia, and refer urgently to the main hospital for surgical management.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Colicky central abdominal pain that shifts to the RIF within 6–12 hours</li>
<li>Constant pain in the RIF</li>
<li>Nausea or vomiting</li>
<li>Fever</li>
<li>Loss of appetite</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2.	 Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Admit patient</li>
<li>Keep nil by mouth (NBM) – do not allow food or drink</li>
<li>Insert IV line and commence fluids</li>
<li>Start IV triple antibiotic therapy</li>
<li>Provide pain relief as required</li>
<li>Organise urgent referral to the main hospital for surgical assessment and management</li>
<li> Haemodynamic instability Call Surgery Critical care Oxygen Iv fluid resuscitation U catheter</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3.	 Clinical assessment</h2>
<p><strong>Abdominal examination findings of severe appendicitis:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Direct tenderness: Pain on pressing in the RIF</li>
<li>Guarding: Voluntary or involuntary tightening of abdominal muscles in the RIF</li>
<li>Rigidity: Board-like abdomen due to reflex spasm of abdominal wall</li>
<li>Rebound tenderness: Pain when pressing firmly for 30 seconds in the RIF and releasing suddenly</li>
<li>Rovsing’s sign: Rebound pain in the RIF when pressing and releasing in the LIF</li>
<li>Psoas sign: Pain in the RIF when patient lifts flexed thigh against resistance</li>
<li>Fever or tachycardia may also be present</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5.	 Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Monitor vital signs and pain severity</li>
<li>Reassess abdominal tenderness</li>
<li>Continue IV antibiotics and fluids</li>
<li>Ensure safe preparation for transfer to surgical unit</li>
<li>Reassess if signs of peritonitis or sepsis develop</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">6.	 Referral / consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Refer urgently to the main hospital surgical team</li>
<li>Escalate immediately if worsening symptoms or generalised peritonitis</li>
<li>Handover medications given, timing of symptoms, and observations during transfer</li>
</ul>
