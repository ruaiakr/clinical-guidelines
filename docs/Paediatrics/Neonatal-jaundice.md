<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Neonatal jaundice (Kiribati Original Protocol)</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Examine all neonates daily for jaundice in the first 3–5 days of life.</li>
<li>Jaundice appearing in the first 24 hours of life or extending to the hands and feet is abnormal and requires urgent assessment.</li>
<li>Encourage frequent breastfeeding (8–12 times per day) to prevent dehydration and reduce bilirubin levels.</li>
<li>Discuss with MO/MA if jaundice is Kramer’s scale &gt;3 or not resolving with sunlight and hydration.</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<p>Jaundice is a yellow discolouration of the skin and sclera caused by elevated serum bilirubin (hyperbilirubinaemia). It affects about 60% of term and 80% of preterm neonates. Most cases are benign; however, high levels of serum bilirubin can cause kernicterus (brain damage or death).</p>
<p><strong>Risk factors include:</strong></p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Preterm birth</li>
<li>Bruising or cephalhaematoma</li>
<li>Poor feeding or dehydration</li>
<li>Sepsis or infection</li>
<li>ABO/Rh incompatibility</li>
<li>Family history of severe jaundice</li>
<li>Birth outside a health facility</li>
<li>Breastfeeding with poor milk intake</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="types-of-hyperbilirubinaemia">Types of hyperbilirubinaemia</h2>
<p><strong>Physiological jaundice</strong> is common.</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>It is due to a physiological increase in bilirubin production with an immature liver to metabolise bilirubin.</li>
<li>Usually presents 24 to 72 hours after birth.</li>
<li>Consider in neonates that are clinically well, have no risk factors for severe jaundice and are zone 4 or less (Kramer’s rule).</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Breastfeeding jaundice</strong> is common in breastfed neonates.</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Physiological jaundice is exacerbated due to sub-optimal breastfeeding or lactation failure which leads to inadequate milk intake.</li>
<li>The neonate may have significant weight loss and be dehydrated.</li>
<li>Usually presents between 24 and 72 hours of life and resolves in 1 to 2 weeks.</li>
<li>Associated with higher risk of severe jaundice and kernicterus.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Breast milk jaundice</strong> is different to breastfeeding jaundice.</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>It usually presents around day 5 of life and persists beyond 2 to 3 weeks of life.</li>
<li>It is thought to be caused by substances in breast milk that reduce breakdown of bilirubin and enhance reabsorption of bilirubin into the bloodstream.</li>
<li>Usually a prolonged but mild jaundice.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Pathological jaundice</strong> is likely if there is:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Jaundice extending to hands and feet.</li>
<li>Jaundice appears within first 24 hours, after first week, or persists &gt; 2 weeks of life.</li>
<li>High level of SBR.</li>
<li>Evidence of conjugated bilirubin present (dark urine).</li>
</ul>
<p>More common underlying conditions that cause pathological jaundice are listed in the table below. Jaundice appearing in the first 24 hours of life usually indicates haemolysis. Haemolytic jaundice occurs with Rhesus (Rh) factor incompatibility, ABO blood group incompatibility, or glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, amongst other more rare conditions.</p>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Yellow skin and sclerae, starting at the head and spreading to trunk, limbs, and extremities</li>
<li>Poor feeding, lethargy, or dehydration</li>
<li>Clinical signs of sepsis if underlying infection present</li>
<li>Weight loss in association with breastfeeding jaundice</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Assess for extent of jaundice using Kramer’s rule (see below)</li>
<li>Ensure adequate feeding — breastfeed 8–12 times per day</li>
<li>Expose the baby to early morning or late afternoon sunlight for 5–10 minutes (naked, with eyes and genitals covered)</li>
<li>If breastfeeding is poor or baby is unwell, feed with expressed breast milk via OGT/NGT</li>
<li>Consider IV fluids only if enteral hydration is inadequate</li>
<li>Discuss with MO/MA if Kramer’s scale &gt;3 or not improving</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Examine the baby in natural daylight daily</li>
<li>Press gently on skin to assess yellowing</li>
<li>Assess hydration status, feeding adequacy, weight trends</li>
<li>Check for signs of sepsis or congenital malaria</li>
<li>Identify any risk factors such as bruising, blood group incompatibility, family history</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="management">4. Management</h2>

<h3 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="hydration-and-feeding">Hydration and feeding</h3>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Breastfeed frequently (8–12 feeds/day)</li>
<li>Use expressed breast milk via OGT/NGT if necessary</li>
<li>Do not give water or sugar water</li>
<li>Start IV fluids only if oral/OGT feeds are insufficient</li>
</ul>

<h3 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="treat-underlying-cause">Treat possible underlying cause</h3>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If infection is suspected, treat for neonatal sepsis (see <a href="/docs/Paediatrics/Neonatal-sepsis">Neonatal sepsis</a>)
<ul>
<li>Ampicillin slow IV (3 min):
<ul>
<li>1 to 7 days
<ul>
<li>&lt;2 kg: 50 mg/kg every 12 hrs</li>
<li>≥2 kg: 50 mg/kg every 8 hrs</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>8 days to &lt;1 month: 50 mg/kg every 8 hrs</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Gentamicin slow IV or IM:
<ul>
<li>&lt;2 kg: 3 mg/kg every 24 hrs</li>
<li>≥2 kg: 5 mg/kg every 24 hrs</li>
<li>Maximum duration: 5 days</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>If congenital malaria suspected, treat per guidelines (see Section 6.6)</li>
</ul>

<h3 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="phototherapy">Phototherapy</h3>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Not currently available in all centres</li>
<li>Refer early if phototherapy or exchange transfusion is required</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="follow-up">5. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Monitor daily during first 3–5 days of life</li>
<li>Observe feeding, hydration, and general activity</li>
<li>Recheck Kramer’s scale and hydration status regularly</li>
<li>Repeat assessment after antibiotics or other treatments if no improvement</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="referral-consultation">6. Referral / Consultation</h2>
<p>Refer urgently to medical officer or higher facility if:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Jaundice appears within first 24 hours of life</li>
<li>Jaundice progresses to hands and feet (Kramer zone 5)</li>
<li>Baby shows signs of sepsis or poor feeding</li>
<li>Jaundice is not resolving with hydration and sunlight</li>
<li>Phototherapy or exchange transfusion is required</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange" id="prevention">Prevention</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Encourage immediate and frequent breastfeeding (8–12 times/day)</li>
<li>Screen for risk factors (e.g. blood group incompatibility, family history)</li>
<li>Examine for jaundice prior to discharge</li>
<li>Educate families on signs of jaundice and when to return for care</li>
</ul>
