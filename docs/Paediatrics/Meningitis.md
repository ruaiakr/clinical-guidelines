# Meningitis – Child

Meningitis – Child
Background
•	 Meningitis is a life-threatening infection of the tissues surrounding the brain
and spinal cord. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency. Rapid diagnosis
and treatment improve outcomes, but mortality remains high in low-resource
settings.
•	 The most common bacterial causes are Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus
influenzae type B, and Neisseria meningitidis. These are vaccine-preventable.
In malnourished or HIV-positive children, gram-negative bacilli (especially
Salmonella) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis are more common. Children with
sickle cell anaemia are more likely to have Salmonella and Staphylococcus aureus
infections.
•	 TB meningitis usually occurs in children under 5 years as a secondary infection
after TB elsewhere in the body. HIV infection increases the risk. Consider TB
meningitis if the child has weight loss, chronic cough, recurrent fevers, night
sweats, or failure to gain weight.
Recommendations
•	 Suspected meningitis must be treated urgently. Start antibiotics immediately.
Provide supportive care and refer to hospital. In neonates and young infants,
signs may be subtle. All suspected cases must be discussed with a doctor.
1.
May present with
In infants and young children:
•	 Fever, vomiting, poor feeding or refusal to suck
•	 Irritability, lethargy, pallor
•	 Apnoea or breathing difficulty
•	 Temperature instability (fever or hypothermia)
•	 Hypoglycaemia without clear cause
•	 Bulging fontanelle
•	 Seizures
•	 Floppy or poor tone
In older children:
•	 Fever, nausea, vomiting, photophobia
•	 Irritability or confusion
•	 Seizures (often generalised)
•	 Headache, neck stiffness (may be absent)
•	 Focal neurological signs (e.g. facial weakness, visual changes)
•	 Petechiae or purpura (suggestive of meningococcal sepsis)
•	 Signs of raised intracranial pressure (e.g. bulging fontanelle, altered
consciousness)

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Use the ABCD approach

–
A: Maintain airway

–
B: Provide oxygen if respiratory distress

–
C: Ensure vascular access and give fluids if needed (2/3 IV maintenance)

–
D: Manage seizures and check blood glucose
•	 Treat fever with paracetamol
•	 Arrange immediate referral to hospital
•	 Begin antibiotics without delay
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Assess temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, capillary refill
•	 Assess general condition and consciousness
•	 Check for signs of raised intracranial pressure
•	 Examine fontanelle in infants
•	 Perform a brief screening physical exam
•	 Doctor will assess for focal neurological signs and plan investigations

5.	 Follow up
•	 Monitor for improvement after starting treatment
•	 If recurrent or chronic: consider HIV or TB and refer for further testing

6.	 Referral/Consultation
•	 Refer immediately to hospital
•	 Consult doctor for lumbar puncture and investigations (FBC, blood culture, LP)
•	 Discuss TB testing if weight loss, chronic cough, or failure to thrive
