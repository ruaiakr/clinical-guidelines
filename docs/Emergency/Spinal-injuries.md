<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Spinal injuries – Adult/Child</h1>
<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend<sup>1</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Focus on minimal handling, maintaining immobilisation and preventing further damage</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Hard/semi-rigid cervical collars are not recommended<sup>1,2</sup></li>
<li>Cervical spine injury is relatively rare in children.2,3 Also see Cervical spine injury<ul><li>emergency management in children www.childrens.health.qld.gov.au/qpec- statewide-guidelines/</li></ul></li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with<sup>1</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>History of trauma, in particular:<ul><li>motor vehicle or motor bike accident - occupant, rider or pedestrian</li><li>industrial accident ie workplace</li><li>dive or jump into shallow water or being ‘dumped’ in the surf</li><li>sporting accident eg rugby, falling from a horse, trampoline<sup>2</sup></li><li>fall from greater than standing height eg ladder, roof</li><li>fall in the elderly</li><li>significant blow to head</li><li>severe penetrating wound eg gunshot</li></ul></li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2.	 Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Do primary survey DRSCABCD as per Traumatic injuries, + initiate any lifesaving measures</li>
<li>Maintain cervical spine (c-spine) in-line immobilisation:1</li>
<li>Do vital signs + BGL</li>
<li>Assess against Criteria for early notification of trauma for interfacility transfer Cervical spine (c-spine) in-line immobilisation</li>
<li>Support head in a neutral position in line with torso - avoid twisting/angular movements</li>
<li>Use soft collar (as prompt to restrict movement) + sand bags to support neck if unconscious<sup>1,2</sup></li>
<li>Padding may help - adult: 2 cm under head child &lt; 8: use thoracic elevation device (TED)<sup>1-3</sup></li>
<li>If conscious, ask them to stay still<sup>1</sup></li>
<li>Restrict spinal movement until patient can undergo appropriate examination ± imaging<sup>4</sup></li>
<li>Lay flat on firm supportive surface C-spine sand-bag support over the soft neck colar pad to immobilise suspected C-spine injury.</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3.	 Clinical assessment<sup>1,4</sup></h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Do secondary survey EFGHIJ as per Traumatic injuries.<ul><li>note mechanism of injury + check for symptoms of spinal injury eg:1</li><li>tingling, numbness in the limbs - where<sup>6</sup> weakness or inability to move the limbs (paralysis)</li><li>altered/absent sensation - where</li><li>nausea, headache or dizziness</li><li>head or neck in abnormal position</li><li>signs of head injury, altered LOC, shock</li><li>breathing difficulties - c-spine or thoracic injury can cause respiratory failure</li><li>change in muscle tone (flaccid/stiff)</li><li>loss of bladder or bowel control</li><li>priapism (persistent erection in males) NEXUS CRITERIA TO ASSESS RISK OF C-SPINE INJURY<sup>4,5</sup> 1 Midline cervical tenderness</li></ul></li>
<li>Neck pain on palpation of the posterior midline neck area, from nuchal ridge to the 3rd thoracic prominence, or palpation of any cervical spinous process 2 Altered mental status</li>
<li>GCS &lt;</li>
<li>Disorientation to time, place, person or event</li>
<li>Inability to remember 3 objects at 5 minutes</li>
<li>Delayed or inappropriate response to external stimuli 3 Focal neurologic deficit</li>
<li>eg motor weakness, numbness/loss of sensation, paraesthesia, other eg priapism<sup>4</sup> 4 Intoxicated - any of</li>
<li>Patient or observer reports recent history of  intoxication/consumption of intoxicating substances</li>
<li>Odour of alcohol, slurred speech, ataxia, lack of coordination, or other cerebellar findings</li>
<li>Behaviour consistent with intoxication</li>
<li>Tests of bodily secretions are positive for drugs or alcohol affecting mental alertness 5 Painful distracting injury</li>
<li>Any non spinal related condition causing sufficient pain to distract patient from a possible cervical spine injury eg:<ul><li>long bone fracture, significant visceral injury</li><li>large laceration, crush or degloving injury, large burns</li><li>other injury that could impair the patient's ability to appreciate cervical spine pain Patient is very unlikely to have c-spine injury if NONE of the above 5 criteria are present AND</li></ul></li>
<li>NO neck pain, bruising or deformity of neck + has full range of active neck movement (45° rotation left and right)<sup>2,4</sup> AND</li>
<li>NO other high risk factors ie:2,6<ul><li>age &gt; 65 (higher incidence of degenerative disease)<sup>4</sup>.6</li><li>fall + landing head first from a height eg diving, trampolining<sup>5</sup></li><li>forced neck hyperflexion (low speed, high force eg rugby scrum collapse)<sup>2</sup></li><li>high risk motor vehicle/bicycle accident eg head on collision, rollover, ejection from vehicle, death in same crash, speed &gt; 88 km/hour<sup>2,4</sup></li><li>substantial torso injury (clavicles, abdomen, flanks, back, spine, pelvis), or thoracic/lumbar spine or face</li></ul></li>
<li>If ANY NEXUS criteria, urgently consult MO - requires evacuation ± CT scan 4,5</li>
<li>Consult MO for clearance before stopping c-spine precautions, regardless of NEXUS criteria<ul><li>generally, if patient is alert, without neurological deficit, sober, does not have additional</li></ul></li>
<li>painful injuries, and has no pain along spine or neck, it is unlikely they have a spinal injury<sup>4</sup></li>
<li>Offer analgesia ± antiemetic. See Acute pain, Nausea and vomiting</li>
<li>If breathing difficulties - monitor SpO<sup>2</sup>, capnography (if able) + VBG</li>
<li>Continue to manage in collaboration with MO until evacuation, which may include:<ul><li>vital signs + neurological observations</li><li>monitoring respiration</li><li>nil by mouth</li><li>keep warm</li><li>IDC ± oro/nasogastric tube<sup>5</sup></li></ul></li>
<li>If hypotensive consider neurogenic shock if hypovolaemia ruled out. Note: haemorrhage is most common cause of shock in trauma<sup>5</sup></li>
<li>If paraplegia or a level of sensory loss on the chest or abdomen, presume spinal instability:4<ul><li>do not try to reduce an obvious deformity<sup>6</sup> Moving patient with suspected spinal injury<sup>1,3</sup></li></ul></li>
<li>Provide in-line immobilisation of the c-spine during transfer and movement of the patient (manually support)</li>
<li>Use scoop stretcher or log roll onto spinal board/vacuum mattress</li>
<li>Do NOT leave on rigid spinal board - high risk of pressure ulcers/necrosis</li>
<li>Use head blocks/sandbags to maintain cervical alignment - DO NOT strap as significant aspiration risk</li>
<li>If extended immobilisation, straighten bedding and remove debris from under patient How to log roll<sup>1</sup>.4 Min. of 3, preferably 5, people needed</li>
<li>1 person takes the lead. They are positioned at the patient's head to provide manual in-line stabilisation (with 2 hands) to the head and neck. This person gives instructions to the rest of the team</li>
<li>3 people (if available) perform the roll:<ul><li>position along the patient's body opposite to the direction that the patient's head is facing</li><li>1 at shoulders/chest; 1 at hips; and 1 in control of the legs</li><li>roll slowly maintaining spinal alignment, avoiding flexion, rotation, lateral bending, sagging of chest or abdomen. Keeping the patient's nose in line with the umbilicus at all times</li></ul></li>
<li>5th person examines back, inserts/removes spinal board etc</li>
</ul>
