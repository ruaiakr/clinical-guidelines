# Ophthalmia neonatorum – neonatal Conjunctivitis (Kiribati Original Protocol)

5.	 Referral / Consultation
•	 Refer urgently if:

–
Signs of necrotising fasciitis

–
Systemic signs of sepsis not improving after 24 hours

–
Recurrent infection

–
No access to IV antibiotics

–
Requires surgical intervention
Prevention
•	 Promote hygienic birth and cord care practices
•	 Educate family to:

–
Keep cord clean and dry

–
Avoid applying substances such as milk, soil, butter, cow dung

–
Report redness, discharge, or swelling early

Ophthalmia neonatorum – neonatal
Conjunctivitis (Kiribati Original Protocol)
Recommend
•	 All neonates with conjunctivitis in the first month of life require prompt
treatment.
•	 Suspect gonococcal or chlamydial conjunctivitis if the mother has untreated or
suspected sexually transmitted infection (STI).
•	 Provide systemic antibiotics for all moderate to severe cases or when STI
pathogens are suspected.
•	 Clean eyes regularly with sterile isotonic solution.
•	 Ensure mother and partner are assessed and treated where relevant.
Background
•	 Ophthalmia neonatorum is an acute conjunctivitis occurring within the first 28
days of life. It may be caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Chlamydia trachomatis,
other skin or gastrointestinal bacteria, or herpes simplex virus. Infection may be
associated with systemic illness or sepsis. Early recognition and treatment are
critical to prevent complications such as corneal ulceration and vision loss.
1.
May present with
Gonococcal conjunctivitis
•	 Onset: 2–7 days after birth
•	 Bilateral purulent discharge
•	 Profuse exudate
•	 Eyelid swelling (oedema)
•	 Risk of corneal ulceration and scarring
Chlamydial conjunctivitis
•	 Onset: 5–14 days after birth
•	 Watery discharge becomes mucopurulent
•	 Unilateral or bilateral involvement
•	 Red, thickened conjunctivae (chemosis)
•	 Pseudomembrane formation may occur
•	 May be associated with pneumonia
Bacterial conjunctivitis
•	 Onset: 2–5 days after birth
•	 Purulent discharge
•	 Similar to gonococcal infection

Herpes simplex keratoconjunctivitis
•	 Onset: second week of life
•	 Watery eyes, red conjunctiva, eye pain
•	 May have skin vesicles
•	 Can progress to cataracts or chorioretinitis
•	 Often part of systemic herpes infection (see Section 6.12.5)
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Clean eyes with 0.9% sodium chloride or Ringer lactate 4 times daily or more
until discharge resolves
•	 If systemic signs (e.g. fever, lethargy, seizures), follow emergency management of
the sick neonate
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Assess eye discharge (timing, character, laterality)
•	 Examine for eyelid swelling, corneal involvement
•	 Look for systemic illness (fever, rash, poor feeding, lethargy)
•	 Review maternal STI history or signs
•	 Record any vesicular lesions (consider herpes)
•	 Check respiratory status (for associated chlamydial pneumonia)
4.	 Management
Gonococcal or bacterial conjunctivitis
•	 Ceftriaxone IM or IV: 50 mg/kg single dose (max 125 mg)
•	 Alternative if jaundice: Cefotaxime IM or IV: 100 mg/kg single dose
If symptoms persist > 48 hours post ceftriaxone, or onset > 7 days of life:
•	 Azithromycin PO: 20 mg/kg once daily for 3 days
•	 Alternative: Erythromycin PO: 12.5 mg/kg 4 times daily for 14 days
Chlamydial conjunctivitis
•	 Azithromycin PO: 20 mg/kg once daily for 3 days
•	 Alternative: Erythromycin PO: 12.5 mg/kg 4 times daily for 14 days
Note: Both azithromycin and erythromycin carry a risk of infantile hypertrophic
pyloric stenosis, especially in the first 2 weeks of life. Monitor for vomiting or
feeding intolerance.
•	 Topical antibiotics are not needed if systemic treatment is given.
