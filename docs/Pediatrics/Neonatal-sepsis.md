# Neonatal sepsis (Kiribati Original Protocol)

Neonatal sepsis (Kiribati Original Protocol)
Recommend
•	 Neonatal sepsis is a life-threatening emergency and must be suspected in any
sick neonate.
•	 Start antibiotics early—do not wait for test results.
•	 Refer urgently to Tungaru Central Hospital (TCH) as soon as neonatal sepsis is
suspected.
•	 While awaiting retrieval or transfer, commence IV antibiotics and fluids.
•	 Ensure the baby is hydrated—insert IV fluids if unable to feed.
Background
•	 Sepsis is a life-threatening condition caused by an uncontrolled response to
infection. In neonates, it may present with subtle or non-specific signs. A low
threshold for starting antibiotics can prevent rapid deterioration.
•	 Common pathogens include Klebsiella spp., E. coli, and Staphylococcus aureus.
Group B Streptococcus and coagulase-negative Staphylococcus are more
common in high-resource settings; Listeria monocytogenes is rare.
1.
May present with
•	 Lethargy or movement only on stimulation
•	 Poor feeding
•	 Fever
•	 Hypothermia (&lt; 35.5°C axillary)
•	 Apnoea or increased apnoeic episodes
•	 Tachycardia or bradycardia
•	 Severe chest indrawing
•	 Delayed capillary refill time (CRT > 2 seconds)
•	 Jaundice, vomiting, diarrhoea, or abdominal distension
•	 Seizures or hypotonia
•	 Irritability
•	 Severe Chest Indrawing

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Call for help and refer to TCH immediately
•	 While awaiting transfer:

–
Keep airway patent (neutral head and neck position)

–
Start oxygen via nasal prongs (aim for SpO₂ 90–95%)

–
Use bag and mask ventilation if RR &lt; 20/min or apnoeic

–
Establish IV access

–
If signs of shock or poor perfusion:
	›
Give IV 0.9% sodium chloride or Ringer lactate 10 mL/kg over 20 minutes
	›
Repeat every 20–30 mins if needed (up to 3 boluses)

–
Check blood glucose level (BGL):
	›
If &lt; 2.5 mmol/L, give slow IV 2 mL/kg of 10% glucose

–
If unable to feed, insert OGT and start IV fluids
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Perform full physical examination and check for localising signs of infection
•	 Check temperature, perfusion, hydration, and neurologic status
•	 Look for signs of:

–
Meningitis or encephalitis

–
Pneumonia

–
Urinary tract infection

–
Omphalitis or soft tissue infection

–
NEC or GI infection

–
Congenital or neonatal malaria
•	 Consider respiratory distress, birth trauma, or perinatal asphyxia as differentials
4.	 Management
First-line antibiotics (start immediately while awaiting referral):
Ampicillin IV slow push (3 minutes) for 7–10 days + Gentamicin IV slow push
(3 minutes) or IM for 5 days

Note: Double the cloxacillin dose for serious infections.
If IV access not possible
•	 Use ceftriaxone IM with caution (especially in preterm or jaundiced neonates)
•	 Alternatively, use IM benzylpenicillin + gentamicin
Duration of antibiotics
•	 7–10 days in most cases
•	 Stop after 5 days if symptoms resolve within 24 hrs
•	 Adjust duration if blood culture results are known (de-escalate if possible)
•	 Refer to meningitis section for extended treatment durations
5.	 Follow up
•	 Continue to monitor while awaiting transfer
•	 Ensure hydration and temperature control
•	 Reassess response to antibiotics daily
•	 Update retrieval team or receiving hospital on clinical status
6.	 Referral / Consultation
•	 Refer immediately to TCH if neonatal sepsis is suspected
•	 Begin antibiotics and fluids before transfer
•	 Continue stabilisation while waiting for retrieval or transport
