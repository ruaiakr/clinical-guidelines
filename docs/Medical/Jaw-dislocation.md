Jaw dislocation – Adult/Child
Background12
Jaw dislocation most commonly involves anterior displacement of the mandible
at the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), typically caused by trauma or excessive
mouth opening (e.g. yawning, dental procedures). It may be unilateral or bilateral.
Prompt recognition and reduction are essential to relieve pain and restore function.
Inappropriate handling can worsen the injury or delay recovery. Dislocations may
recur in individuals with underlying joint laxity or structural abnormalities.
Recommend12
•	 Jaw dislocation is typically an anterior displacement of the mandible.
•	 Prompt reduction is important to relieve pain, restore function, and prevent
complications.
•	 Avoid repeated manipulation – refer early if uncertain.
1.
May present with12
•	 History of trauma, yawning, laughing, or wide mouth opening
•	 Pain in front of ear(s) or jaw
•	 Inability to close mouth
•	 Jaw appears displaced forward, difficulty speaking
•	 Drooling, anxiety, or distress
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Reassure patient and support jaw with gentle pressure
•	 Avoid forcing jaw movement
•	 Place patient in sitting or semi-reclined position
•	 Provide analgesia and refer promptly if in distress or reduction not possible
3.	 Clinical assessment123
•	 Take history: mechanism of injury, previous dislocations, recent dental procedures
•	 Check for:

–
Symmetry of jaw

–
Palpable depression in preauricular area

–
Inability to occlude teeth

–
Associated injuries (e.g. head, cervical spine)
•	 Assess for signs of fracture or nerve injury (numbness, weakness)
•	 Examine ears and oral cavity
•	 Assess airway and ability to swallow/speak

b. Reduction (if trained)45
Only attempt if trained and confident; otherwise refer.
•	 Wear gloves, sit or stand facing patient.
•	 Patient seated upright, mouth slightly open.
•	 Wrap gauze around both thumbs and place on lower molars inside mouth.
•	 Rest fingers under chin.
•	 Apply downward and backward pressure until you feel the jaw ‘pop’ into place.
•	 Immediately support jaw with a bandage (e.g. Barton bandage) or instruct
patient to keep mouth closed.

5.	 Follow up
•	 Avoid wide mouth opening for 2 weeks (e.g. yawning, large bites)
•	 Soft diet for several days
•	 Educate patient on recurrence risk
•	 Monitor for signs of fracture, nerve damage, or joint dysfunction
•	 Refer for imaging or specialist if:

–
Recurrent dislocations

–
Suspected fracture

–
Reduction unsuccessful
6.	 Referral/consultation
Refer urgently if:
•	 Unable to reduce or unsure of diagnosis
•	 Suspected mandibular fracture
•	 Associated head injury or airway compromise
•	 Dislocation complicated by seizure, trauma, or high-risk comorbidity
MHMS to advise if ongoing instability or access to imaging/specialist is required.
