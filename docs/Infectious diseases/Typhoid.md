Typhoid
Background
Typhoid fever, also ‘Enteric Fever’ is a bacterial infection caused by Salmonella
enterica serotype Typhi (formerly S. typhi). It is transmitted through contaminated
food or water and is common in areas with inadequate sanitation. Humans are the
only reservoir for S. typhi. It can lead to severe illness if not recognized and treated
promptly with antibiotics.
Recommendations
•	 A notifiable disease and should be reported if suspected to Public Health.
•	 Ensure access to clean water and proper sanitation to reduce transmission.
•	 Typhoid vaccination is recommended for high-risk populations.
•	 Early diagnosis and appropriate antibiotic therapy are essential to prevent
complications.
1.
May present with
•	 High fever (often > 39°C)
•	 Abdominal pain, especially in the right lower quadrant
•	 Headache and fatigue
•	 Diarrhoea or constipation
Case Definition when serology is not available:
Fever ≥38°C for ≥3 days plus two or more of:
•	 Abdominal pain
•	 Headache
•	 Weakness or malaise
•	 Constipation or diarrhoea
•	 Coated tongue or dry mouth
•	 Relative bradycardia
•	 Enlarged liver/spleen
•	 Rose spots (rash on trunk)
Probable case:
•	 Suspected case with an epidemiological link (e.g. recent travel to outbreak area
or contact with confirmed case)
Confirmed case:
•	 Laboratory confirmation (culture or serology, if available)
Public Health Action:
•	 Notify public health authorities; reinforce hygiene; document close contacts.

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Start empirical antibiotic therapy with fluoroquinolones or third-generation
cephalosporins.
•	 Ensure the patient is hydrated and monitor for signs of dehydration or septic
shock.
•	 Isolate the patient to reduce transmission to others.

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Conduct a detailed history to assess risk factors such as travel to endemic areas.
•	 Perform a blood culture to confirm diagnosis.
•	 Monitor for complications such as intestinal perforation or septicaemia.
4.	 Management
•	 Administer appropriate antibiotics based on local susceptibility patterns.
•	 Provide symptomatic care, including antipyretics and fluids for hydration.
•	 Monitor for complications and adjust treatment as necessary.
5.	 Follow-up
•	 Regular follow-up to ensure resolution of fever and other symptoms.
•	 Monitor for relapse, which can occur in a small percentage of cases.
6.	 Referral/Consultation
•	 Refer to a specialist if complications such as intestinal perforation, septic shock,
or drug resistance are suspected.
