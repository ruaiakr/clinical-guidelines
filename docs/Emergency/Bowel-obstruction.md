Management of common surgical abdominal
condition associated with acute abdominal pain
Bowel obstruction (large and small bowel)
Background
Intestinal obstruction is an important cause of acute abdomen and accounts for
approximately 10% of emergency surgical admissions. It occurs when the normal
flow of intestinal contents is interrupted, either partially or completely.
Obstruction can occur in the small or large bowel and may be classified in several
ways:
•	 Permanent or intermittent
•	 Complete or partial
•	 Acute or chronic
•	 Intrinsic or extrinsic
•	 Mechanical or functional
Mortality varies depending on the cause and associated complications. Mortality
is 100% in untreated strangulated obstructions. Morbidity and mortality are
significantly increased in patients where diagnosis is delayed or management is
inappropriate.
Common causes include:
•	 Volvulus
•	 Strangulated hernias
•	 Faecal impaction (e.g. Hirschsprung’s disease)
•	 Neoplastic causes (e.g. bowel cancer)
Recommendations
All patients with suspected bowel obstruction must be admitted for close
monitoring and stabilisation. Initiate IV fluids, insert a nasogastric tube for
decompression, commence broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, and arrange urgent
referral to the main hospital for definitive investigation and management.
1.
May present with
• Abdominal pain
• Abdominal distension or bloating
• Nausea and vomiting
• Constipation or obstipation (no passage of faeces or flatus)

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Admit patient
•	 Monitor vital signs regularly
•	 Keep nil by mouth (NBM)
•	 Insert nasogastric tube (NGT) for decompression and free drainage
•	 Begin IV fluid resuscitation as per weight
•	 Start IV broad-spectrum antibiotics (e.g. Ampicillin + Metronidazole)
•	 Organise urgent referral to main hospital for surgical review and further
management
3.	 Clinical assessment
General appearance
•	 Patient may or may not show signs of distress
•	 Blood pressure and pulse may be elevated
Abdominal examination
•	 Abdominal distension
•	 Visible or obvious bowel peristalsis
•	 Tense and tender abdomen
•	 Bowel sounds:

–
High-pitched or “tinkling” in early obstruction

–
Reduced or absent in late obstruction or paralytic ileus
