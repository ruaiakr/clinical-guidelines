Anemia - Adult/Child
Recommend
Anaemia is a condition where the number of red blood cells or the amount
of haemoglobin is below normal. It is not a diagnosis in itself but a sign of an
underlying cause that must be identified and treated.
Normal Haemoglobin Ranges
•	 Adults

–
Males: 13–18 g/dL

–
Females: 12–16 g/dL
•	 Children

–
Birth: 13.5 g/dL

–
6 weeks: 9.5 g/dL

–
3 months: 10 g/dL

–
6 months – 1.5 years: 11 g/dL

–
1.5 – 7 years: 10.5 g/dL

–
7 – 12 years: 11.5 g/dL
Types of Anaemia
Anaemia is not a diagnosis in itself—there is always an underlying cause which must
be determined before treatment. The main types of anaemia include:
Deficiency Anaemia
Caused by a lack of substances essential for blood production:
•	 Iron deficiency (most
common):

–
Caused by blood loss (e.g. trauma, ectopic pregnancy, childbirth, excessive
menstruation, bleeding gastric ulcer)

–
Medications such as aspirin, ibuprofen, and indomethacin may cause gastric
ulcers and bleeding

–
Inadequate dietary intake of iron (common in malnutrition)

–
 Chronic helminth infection and colon cancer can also cause chronic bleeding
•	 Folic acid deficiency:

–
Causes megaloblastic anaemia (red blood cells are larger than normal)

–
Common in pregnancy, infancy, liver disease, and malabsorption disorders
•	 Vitamin B12 deficiency:

–
Results in pernicious anaemia due to impaired absorption

–
Causes include gastrectomy, absence of intrinsic factor, or malabsorption (e.g.
non-tropical sprue with villous atrophy)
•	 Vitamin C deficiency:

–
Leads to scurvy, which can be associated with anaemia

Haemolytic Anaemia
•	 Due to excessive destruction of red blood cells
•	 May occur in conditions like sickle cell disease
Anaemia due to Bone Marrow Disorders
•	 Aplastic anaemia: failure of the bone marrow to produce red cells
•	 Leukaemia: cancer of the blood-forming tissues
•	 Drug-induced: some antibiotics (e.g. chloramphenicol), anti-epileptics, and oral
diabetic medications can suppress the bone marrow
•	 Exposure to poisons may also impair red cell production
Anaemia of Chronic Disease
•	 Seen in chronic conditions such as:

–
Renal failure

–
Tuberculosis

–
Chronic liver disease

–
Thyroid disorders

–
Rheumatoid arthritis

–
Malignancies (cancer)
1.
May present with
•	 Fatigue, pallor, weakness
•	 Shortness of breath, palpitations
•	 Dizziness, fainting, headache
•	 Poor feeding or growth (children)
•	 Irritability or behavioural changes
•	 Delayed development or school problems• Heavy or prolonged menstruation
(women)
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Check haemoglobin using lab or haemoglobinometer if available
•	 Assess and triage – check vital signs
•	 If signs of severe anaemia (extreme pallor, fainting, rapid breathing), call for help
and consult MO urgently
•	 Stop contributing medications (e.g. NSAIDs, aspirin)
•	 Deworm all patients:

–
Albendazole 400 mg stat, repeat after 1 week

–
OR Mebendazole 100 mg twice daily for 3 days, repeat after 1 week

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 History: recent or chronic bleeding, nutrition, medication, chronic illness
•	 Full physical exam:

–
Skin, conjunctiva, palms for pallor

–
Liver, spleen, lymph nodes

–
Rectal/pelvic exam in adults if bleeding suspected

–
Growth chart and milestones in children
•	 Ask about:

–
Menorrhagia, post-partum bleeding

–
Abdominal pain or past surgeries

–
Diet and malnutrition

–
Chronic infections (TB, liver or kidney disease)
4.	 Management
•	 If Hb low and likely cause is known (e.g. iron deficiency), treat accordingly
•	 If due to deficiency of iron, folate or vitamin B12 – give supplementation and treat
cause
•	 The underlying cause must be identified (e.g. bleeding, malabsorption, chronic
disease)
Iron therapy (first-line)
•	 Adults:

–
Ferrous sulphate 300 mg orally twice daily after meals

–
Once Hb is normal, reduce to 1 tablet daily × 6 months
•	 Children:

–
Dose as per age and weight – typically 3–6 mg/kg/day of elemental iron
divided twice daily

–
Include folic acid and Vitamin C
•	 Pregnancy:

–
Ferrous sulphate with folic acid (200 mg iron + 350 mcg folate) – 1 tablet daily
throughout pregnancy
Add-on treatments
•	 Vitamin C – 100 mg orally 3
times daily
•	 Vitamin A – 200,000 IU orally
stat in malnourished children
•	 Folic acid – Add if poor diet,
pregnancy, or megaloblastic anaemia suspected
•	 Vitamin B12 – Only on doctor
advice, for confirmed B12 deficiency

Dietary advice
•	 Encourage iron-rich foods: liver, meat, fish, leafy greens, legumes, eggs
•	 Give small, frequent meals in children
If no improvement
•	 Hb should rise ~1g/dL per week
•	 If no improvement or relapse after treatment, refer to hospital
•	 If patient becomes symptomatic or has unclear cause, refer to MO
5.	 Follow up
•	 Weekly review for 1 month
•	 Monitor clinical improvement and weight gain (children)
•	 Reinforce adherence to iron and diet
•	 Continue therapy for 3–6 months to rebuild iron stores
6.	 Referral/Consultation
•	 If severe anaemia, no improvement after 2–3 weeks, or cause unclear
•	 If symptoms worsen (e.g. fainting, breathlessness)
•	 Suspected bone marrow disorders, leukaemia, or chronic disease
•	 Children with delayed development or failure to thrive despite treatment
