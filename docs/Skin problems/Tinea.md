# Tinea (ringworm, athlete’s foot, jock itch, scalp ringworm, fungal nail infection)

Skin problems

Tinea (ringworm, athlete’s foot, jock itch,
scalp ringworm, fungal nail infection)
Recommend
Treat tinea infections early with antifungal cream. Refer if infection is widespread,
involves scalp or nails, or if not responding to topical treatment. Treat secondary
bacterial infection first if present.
Background
Tinea is a fungal infection of the skin, scalp, groin, feet, or nails. It includes different
types such as tinea corporis (body), tinea faciale (face), tinea capitis (scalp), tinea
cruris (groin), tinea pedis (feet), and onychomycosis (nails). Spread occurs through
direct contact with infected people, animals, or contaminated items. It is more
common in hot, humid conditions and among immunocompromised people.
1.
May present with
Ringworm (e.g. tinea corporis, tinea faciale)
•	 One or more red ring-shaped patches or plaques
•	 Central clearing as they expand
•	 Well-defined, slightly raised, sometimes scaly borders
•	 Commonly on trunk, face, arms, or legs
•	 Often itchy
Scalp ringworm / Tinea capitis
•	 Itchy, scaly plaques or patches on scalp
•	 Patches of hair loss, black dots from broken hairs
•	 May present as a boggy, pustular mass (kerion)
•	 Most common in children aged 3–7 years
Jock itch / Tinea cruris
•	 Red, itchy, scaly rash on upper thigh and groin
•	 Usually spares the scrotum
•	 More common in adolescent and adult males
•	 Risk factors: sweating, tight clothing, immunocompromised state
Athlete’s foot / Tinea pedis
•	 Itching or burning feet, often with odour
•	 Scaling, maceration, or sloughing between toes
•	 May spread to toenails

Fungal nail infection / Onychomycosis
•	 Nail discolouration, thickening
•	 Separation of nail from nail bed
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Not applicable unless there are signs of a secondary bacterial infection – treat
these first (see Impetigo or Cellulitis)
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Take a history:

–
Onset and duration

–
Itching, burning, tenderness

–
Prior infections or treatments

–
Animal contact

–
Immunocompromised or diabetic status
•	 Check vital signs
•	 Examine affected skin – see Skin assessment – adult or child
•	 If tinea cruris (jock itch), check feet as a possible source
•	 Consider differential diagnosis: Candidiasis (skin), eczema, dermatitis, psoriasis
•	 Look for signs of secondary bacterial infection:

–
Red, tender, glistening or weeping skin, crusts
4.	 Management
•	 If secondary bacterial infection, treat first – see Impetigo, or Cellulitis
•	 Refer to MO clinic if:

–
Widespread skin involvement

–
Involves scalp, nails, palms or soles

–
Immunocompromised patient

–
Recurrent or unresponsive to topical treatment
•	 MO may prescribe oral antifungal treatment (e.g. Griseofulvin, Terbinafine) and
request fungal MCS (skin scrapings from edge, nail clippings, or plucked hair)
•	 Treat all other tinea infections with antifungal creams:

–
Start with Myconazole cream for up to 3 weeks

–
May also use: Ketoconazole shampoo, Griseofulvin 500 mg orally once daily
for 4 weeks, Itraconazole, or Fluconazole
•	 Provide prevention advice:

–
Keep affected areas dry (e.g. loose clothing, dry well between toes)

–
Avoid walking barefoot in public showers

–
Dry moist footwear in the sun

–
Improve diabetes or weight control if relevant

–
Avoid contact with infected pets or farm animals

–
Avoid sharing personal items (e.g. combs, towels, footwear, bedding)
