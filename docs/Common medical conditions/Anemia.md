<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Anemia - Adult/Child</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Recommend</h2>
<p>Anaemia is a condition where the number of red blood cells or the amount of haemoglobin is below normal. It is not a diagnosis in itself but a sign of an underlying cause that must be identified and treated.</p>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Normal Haemoglobin Ranges</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Adults</strong>
<ul>
<li>Males: 13–18 g/dL</li>
<li>Females: 12–16 g/dL</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Children</strong>
<ul>
<li>Birth: 13.5 g/dL</li>
<li>6 weeks: 9.5 g/dL</li>
<li>3 months: 10 g/dL</li>
<li>6 months – 1.5 years: 11 g/dL</li>
<li>1.5 – 7 years: 10.5 g/dL</li>
<li>7 – 12 years: 11.5 g/dL</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Types of Anaemia</h2>
<p>Anaemia is not a diagnosis in itself—there is always an underlying cause which must be determined before treatment. The main types of anaemia include:</p>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Deficiency Anaemia</h2>
<p>Caused by a lack of substances essential for blood production:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Iron deficiency</strong> (most common):
<ul>
<li>Caused by blood loss (e.g. trauma, ectopic pregnancy, childbirth, excessive menstruation, bleeding gastric ulcer)</li>
<li>Medications such as aspirin, ibuprofen, and indomethacin may cause gastric ulcers and bleeding</li>
<li>Inadequate dietary intake of iron (common in malnutrition)</li>
<li>Chronic helminth infection and colon cancer can also cause chronic bleeding</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Folic acid deficiency:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Causes megaloblastic anaemia (red blood cells are larger than normal)</li>
<li>Common in pregnancy, infancy, liver disease, and malabsorption disorders</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin B12 deficiency:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Results in pernicious anaemia due to impaired absorption</li>
<li>Causes include gastrectomy, absence of intrinsic factor, or malabsorption (e.g. non-tropical sprue with villous atrophy)</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Vitamin C deficiency:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Leads to scurvy, which can be associated with anaemia</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Haemolytic Anaemia</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Due to excessive destruction of red blood cells</li>
<li>May occur in conditions like sickle cell disease</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Anaemia due to Bone Marrow Disorders</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Aplastic anaemia:</strong> failure of the bone marrow to produce red cells</li>
<li><strong>Leukaemia:</strong> cancer of the blood-forming tissues</li>
<li><strong>Drug-induced:</strong> some antibiotics (e.g. chloramphenicol), anti-epileptics, and oral diabetic medications can suppress the bone marrow</li>
<li>Exposure to poisons may also impair red cell production</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Anaemia of Chronic Disease</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Seen in chronic conditions such as:
<ul>
<li>Renal failure</li>
<li>Tuberculosis</li>
<li>Chronic liver disease</li>
<li>Thyroid disorders</li>
<li>Rheumatoid arthritis</li>
<li>Malignancies (cancer)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">1. May present with</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Fatigue, pallor, weakness</li>
<li>Shortness of breath, palpitations</li>
<li>Dizziness, fainting, headache</li>
<li>Poor feeding or growth (children)</li>
<li>Irritability or behavioural changes</li>
<li>Delayed development or school problems</li>
<li>Heavy or prolonged menstruation (women)</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Check haemoglobin using lab or haemoglobinometer if available</li>
<li>Assess and triage – check vital signs</li>
<li>If signs of severe anaemia (extreme pallor, fainting, rapid breathing), call for help and consult MO urgently</li>
<li>Stop contributing medications (e.g. NSAIDs, aspirin)</li>
<li>Deworm all patients:
<ul>
<li>Albendazole 400 mg stat, repeat after 1 week</li>
<li><strong>OR</strong> Mebendazole 100 mg twice daily for 3 days, repeat after 1 week</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>History: recent or chronic bleeding, nutrition, medication, chronic illness</li>
<li>Full physical exam:
<ul>
<li>Skin, conjunctiva, palms for pallor</li>
<li>Liver, spleen, lymph nodes</li>
<li>Rectal/pelvic exam in adults if bleeding suspected</li>
<li>Growth chart and milestones in children</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Ask about:
<ul>
<li>Menorrhagia, post-partum bleeding</li>
<li>Abdominal pain or past surgeries</li>
<li>Diet and malnutrition</li>
<li>Chronic infections (TB, liver or kidney disease)</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">4. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If Hb low and likely cause is known (e.g. iron deficiency), treat accordingly</li>
<li>If due to deficiency of iron, folate or vitamin B12 – give supplementation and treat cause</li>
<li>The underlying cause must be identified (e.g. bleeding, malabsorption, chronic disease)</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Iron therapy (first-line)</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Adults:
<ul>
<li>Ferrous sulphate 300 mg orally twice daily after meals</li>
<li>Once Hb is normal, reduce to 1 tablet daily × 6 months</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Children:
<ul>
<li>Dose as per age and weight – typically 3–6 mg/kg/day of elemental iron divided twice daily</li>
<li>Include folic acid and Vitamin C</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>Pregnancy:
<ul>
<li>Ferrous sulphate with folic acid (200 mg iron + 350 mcg folate) – 1 tablet daily throughout pregnancy</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Add-on treatments</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Vitamin C – 100 mg orally 3 times daily</li>
<li>Vitamin A – 200,000 IU orally stat in malnourished children</li>
<li>Folic acid – Add if poor diet, pregnancy, or megaloblastic anaemia suspected</li>
<li>Vitamin B12 – Only on doctor advice, for confirmed B12 deficiency</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">Dietary advice</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Encourage iron-rich foods: liver, meat, fish, leafy greens, legumes, eggs</li>
<li>Give small, frequent meals in children</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-orange">If no improvement</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Hb should rise ~1 g/dL per week</li>
<li>If no improvement or relapse after treatment, refer to hospital</li>
<li>If patient becomes symptomatic or has unclear cause, refer to MO</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">5. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Weekly review for 1 month</li>
<li>Monitor clinical improvement and weight gain (children)</li>
<li>Reinforce adherence to iron and diet</li>
<li>Continue therapy for 3–6 months to rebuild iron stores</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">6. Referral/Consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>If severe anaemia, no improvement after 2–3 weeks, or cause unclear</li>
<li>If symptoms worsen (e.g. fainting, breathlessness)</li>
<li>Suspected bone marrow disorders, leukaemia, or chronic disease</li>
<li>Children with delayed development or failure to thrive despite treatment</li>
</ul>
</div>
