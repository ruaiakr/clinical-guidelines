<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Odontogenic infection and spread to neck spaces (Kiribati Original Protocol)</h1>

<div class="pccm-guideline-panel">
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">Background</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Odontogenic infections arise from dental or oral pathology and can lead to serious systemic complications. If left untreated, these infections can spread to adjacent tissues, including the neck spaces, leading to severe conditions such as Ludwig’s Angina. Early detection and prompt management are essential to prevent airway compromise, sepsis, and other life-threatening consequences.</li>
</ul>
<h2 class="pccm-section-heading">General Principles</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Follow standard infection control protocols to prevent cross-contamination and ensure patient safety.</li>
<li>Recognize red flags and symptoms that require urgent referral.</li>
<li>Provide early pain management and antibiotics as indicated.</li>
<li>Always ensure airway management is prioritized, especially if neck space involvement is suspected.</li>
<li>Conduct a full assessment and monitor for any signs of systemic infection or airway compromise.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="may-present-with">1. May present with</h2>
<p>Odontogenic infections may present with the following symptoms, which could indicate spread to the neck spaces:</p>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Swelling and erythema of the lower face, neck, or jaw.</li>
<li>Severe pain in the affected area.</li>
<li>Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) or painful swallowing.</li>
<li>Trismus (inability to open the mouth fully).</li>
<li>Fever and chills suggesting systemic infection.</li>
<li>Difficulty breathing or hoarseness (suggesting airway compromise).</li>
<li>Swelling of the tongue or floor of the mouth.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="immediate-management">2. Immediate management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Airway Management:</strong> Always assess the airway first. In cases of Ludwig’s Angina, where airway obstruction is a risk, early intervention to secure the airway is critical. Consider intubation or emergency surgical intervention if necessary.</li>
<li><strong>Analgesia:</strong> Administer appropriate pain relief, considering the severity of the infection and the patient’s overall condition. Opioid analgesics may be required for severe pain.</li>
<li><strong>Antibiotics:</strong> Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics should be administered early. Common regimens include a combination of penicillin, metronidazole, or clindamycin to cover both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.</li>
<li><strong>Hydration and Fluid Management:</strong> Administer IV fluids as necessary to maintain hydration and support systemic circulation.</li>
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Close monitoring of vital signs, including temperature, pulse, and oxygen saturation, is essential to detect signs of sepsis or airway compromise early.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="clinical-assessment">3. Clinical assessment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>History:</strong> Obtain a detailed history of the symptoms, including the onset and progression of pain, fever, and swelling. Ask about recent dental procedures or infections.</li>
<li><strong>Examination:</strong> Perform a thorough examination of the oral cavity, including palpation of the floor of the mouth, submandibular regions, and neck. Assess for signs of swelling, erythema, and tenderness.</li>
<li><strong>Neurological Examination:</strong> Check for signs of infection affecting the cranial nerves, particularly the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, which could signal involvement of deep neck spaces.</li>
<li><strong>Functional Assessment:</strong> Evaluate the patient’s ability to open the mouth, swallow, and breathe comfortably. Difficulty opening the mouth or swallowing could be an indication of severe infection or airway compromise.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="management">4. Management</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Conservative Management:</strong> For mild odontogenic infections, oral antibiotics and analgesics may be sufficient. Follow up with dental or oral health specialists as necessary.</li>
<li><strong>Surgical Intervention:</strong> Surgical drainage may be necessary for deep infections or when there is abscess formation. In severe cases, Ludwig’s angina or deep neck infections may require neck exploration or debridement.</li>
<li><strong>Referral:</strong>
<ul>
<li>Refer patients with signs of airway compromise or severe systemic infection to the emergency department for immediate intervention.</li>
<li>Refer to an oral and maxillofacial specialist or ENT surgeon for definitive management, especially if the infection has spread to deep neck spaces.</li>
</ul>
</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="follow-up">5. Follow up</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Monitoring:</strong> Patients should be closely monitored for any signs of worsening infection or airway obstruction. If the patient requires hospitalization, frequent reassessment is necessary.</li>
<li><strong>Rehabilitation:</strong> In severe cases, follow-up may include physical therapy for jaw mobility or speech therapy, depending on the extent of the infection and its impact on the oral and pharyngeal structures.</li>
<li><strong>Psychological Support:</strong> Provide psychological support for patients dealing with the stress or anxiety of managing a potentially life-threatening condition.</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue" id="referral-consultation">6. Referral/consultation</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li><strong>Urgent Referral:</strong> For patients with airway compromise, signs of sepsis, or those who require surgical drainage or debridement.</li>
<li><strong>Routine Referral:</strong> For patients with early-stage odontogenic infections or those not showing signs of severe systemic involvement.</li>
<li><strong>Specialist Consultation:</strong> Consult with an oral and maxillofacial surgeon, ENT specialist, or infectious disease expert as necessary.</li>
</ul>
