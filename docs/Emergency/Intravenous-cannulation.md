<h1 class="pccm-page-title">Intravenous (IV) cannulation</h1>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">General considerations</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Insertion of a cannula into a vein to administer fluids or medications</li>
<li>Common in emergency and elective settings</li>
<li>Requires good knowledge of anatomy, technique, and asepsis</li>
<li>It is recommended to choose a straight portion of a vein to minimize the chance of hitting valves</li>
<li>Use the patient’s non-dominant arm (if possible)</li>
<li>For prolonged courses of therapy, it is recommended to start distally and move proximally as distal catheters are replaced</li>
<li>The superficial veins of the upper extremities are preferred to those of the lower extremities for peripheral venous access as they interfere less with patient mobility and pose a lower risk for phlebitis</li>
<li><strong>DO NOT INSERT A CANNULA IN AN ARTERY</strong> – risk of limb ischemia</li>
<li>In a diabetic patient, avoid lower limb cannulation</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">Venous access allows</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Sampling of blood</li>
<li>Administration of IV fluids and medications</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">Contraindications</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>No absolute contraindications to intravenous cannulation exist</li>
<li>Avoid peripheral venous access in an injured, infected, or burned extremity if possible</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">Equipment</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Non-sterile gloves</li>
<li>Tourniquet</li>
<li>Antiseptic wipes</li>
<li>5-ml syringe</li>
<li>Sterile gauze</li>
<li>Cannula</li>
<li>Saline flush</li>
<li>Adhesive plaster</li>
</ul>

<h2 class="pccm-section-heading-blue">Veins commonly used for IV access</h2>
<ul class="pccm-content-list">
<li>Cephalic vein (thumb side)</li>
<li>Basilic vein (little finger side)</li>
<li>Median cubital vein (elbow crease)</li>
<li>Dorsal veins of the hand</li>
</ul>
