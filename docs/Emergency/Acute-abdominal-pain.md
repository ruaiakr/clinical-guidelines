Acute abdominal pain
Background
Acute abdomen refers to a sudden onset of severe abdominal pain that may
require urgent medical attention. The cause can be gastrointestinal, urological,
reproductive, respiratory, or related to trauma. A diagram classifying causes of
acute abdominal pain should be inserted here.
In females of reproductive age, always consider:
•	 Ectopic pregnancy –
particularly if pain is accompanied by vaginal bleeding
•	 Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) – particularly if &lt;30 years and sexually active,
with new pelvic pain
In males:
•	 Testicular torsion must be considered in all males with abdominal pain
•	 In male infants with inconsolable crying, examine for testicular or scrotal
pathology
A definitive diagnosis is not always required immediately. However, a detailed and
structured history and examination are critical to guide next steps and identify red
flags.
Recommendations
Always take a comprehensive pain history and perform a full abdominal and genital
examination. Urgently consult or refer patients with red flags such as signs of
peritonitis, sepsis, shock, or suspected ectopic pregnancy or testicular torsion.
1.
May present with
•	 Sudden or gradual onset of abdominal pain
•	 Pain may be continuous or intermittent
•	 Pain may be localised or generalised
•	 Pain may radiate to the back or shoulder tip
•	 Associated symptoms may include:

–
Nausea or vomiting (bile, blood, or faecal-smelling)

–
Diarrhoea or constipation

–
Fever or shortness of breath

–
Dysuria, haematuria

–
Vaginal bleeding or discharge

–
Poor feeding, refusal to eat (children)

–
Recent trauma to abdomen, scrotum, or genital area

–
Weight loss or anorexia

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Apply DRSABCD approach
•	 If unstable: start IV access and fluids
•	 Keep nil by mouth
•	 Provide pain relief as needed
•	 If testicular torsion or ectopic pregnancy suspected: urgent referral
•	 Monitor vital signs
•	 Do not delay consultation in unstable patients or those with red flags
3.	 Clinical assessment
History of pain
Ask about:
•	 Site – ask patient to point to location
•	 Onset – sudden, gradual; what were they doing when it started
•	 Character – sharp, dull, stabbing, crampy, burning
•	 Radiation – e.g. to back, shoulder tip
•	 Timing – first onset, duration, prior episodes
•	 Exacerbating/alleviating factors – worse with movement? relieved by lying still?
•	 Severity – scale 1–10, impact on sleep or daily activity
Associated symptoms
Ask about:
•	 Vomiting – bile, blood, green, faecal odour
•	 Bowel habits – diarrhoea, constipation, black or bloody stools, last motion
•	 Urine – frequency, burning, urgency, blood
•	 Vaginal – bleeding, discharge
•	 Respiratory symptoms – shortness of breath, cough (to consider pneumonia)
•	 Weight loss, poor feeding, food refusal
•	 Foreign body ingestion or recent trauma (especially in children)
Physical examination
Perform a full abdominal examination:
•	 Look:

–
Shape and symmetry (distended, sunken)

–
Surgical scars, bruises, hernias, distended veins

–
Signs of non-accidental injury
•	 Palpate:

–
Start away from site of pain; light then deep palpation in 9 areas

–
Assess for rebound tenderness (pain when pressure is released)

–
Localised tenderness, distension, guarding (involuntary tightening)

–
Pain out of proportion to findings

–
Palpate kidneys with one hand under flank and one above

5.	 Follow up
•	 Reassess regularly, especially if diagnosis unclear
•	 Monitor vital signs and symptoms closely
•	 Repeat abdominal examination if symptoms persist or worsen
•	 If diagnosis changes, adjust management plan and refer if needed
6.	 Referral / consultation
•	 Urgent referral to main hospital or surgical unit if:

–
Signs of peritonitis, sepsis, or shock

–
Suspected ectopic pregnancy or testicular torsion

–
Persistent pain with unclear diagnosis
•	 Consult for review if any red flag symptoms are present
