# Dog Bite – all ages (Kiribati Original Protocol)

Dog Bite – all ages (Kiribati Original Protocol)
GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS:
•	 Dog bites can cause serious injury and infection, including tetanus, sepsis, and
rabies (if rabies is endemic).
•	 Early assessment and wound care are essential to reduce complications.
•	 Tetanus toxoid (TT) should be given based on immunisation history and wound
risk.
•	 In rabies-endemic areas, rabies post-exposure prophylaxis may be needed.
•	 Bitten by a dog (domestic or wild), may or may not break the skin
1.
May present with 1,3
•	 Skin puncture, laceration or crush injury from dog bite
•	 Bleeding, swelling, redness, or signs of infection (pain, pus, fever)
•	 History of animal attack or close contact with saliva through broken skin
•	 Anxiety or distress, especially in children
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Apply basic first aid: control bleeding and clean the wound with soap and
running water for at least 15 minutes
•	 Remove any foreign material if visible
•	 Cover with a clean dressing
•	 Reassure the patient and caregiver
•	 Urgently assess TT status (see table on following pages)
3.	 Clinical assessment 1,2,3
•	 Obtain history:

–
Time and location of the bite

–
Whether dog is domestic or stray, vaccinated, or known to the patient

–
Provoked or unprovoked attack

–
TT and rabies immunisation history
	›
Assess wound:

–
Site, depth, contamination, and evidence of infection

–
High-risk sites: face, hands, feet, genitals
	›
Do full vital signs
	›
Consider mental health impact (particularly in children)

Consider the Dunbar Scale to assess the levl of bite severity and whether the
patient needs review by the surgical team: An assessment of the severity of biting
problems based on an objective evaluation of wound pathology
•	 Level 1. Obnoxious or aggressive behavior but no skin-contact by teeth.
•	 Level 2. Skin-contact by teeth but no skin-puncture. However, may be skin nicks
(less than one tenth of an inch deep) and slight bleeding caused by forward or
lateral movement of teeth against skin, but no vertical punctures.
•	 Level 3. One to four punctures from a single bite with no puncture deeper than
half the length of the dog’s canine teeth. Maybe lacerations in a single direction,
caused by victim pulling hand away, owner pulling dog away, or gravity (little
dog jumps, bites and drops to floor).
•	 Level 4. One to four punctures from a single bite with at least one puncture
deeper than half the length of the dog’s canine teeth. May also have deep
bruising around the wound (dog held on for N seconds and bore down) or
lacerations in both directions (dog held on and shook its head from side to side).
•	 Level 5. Multiple-bite incident with at least two Level 4 bites or multiple-attack
incident with at least one Level 4 bite in each.
•	 Level 6. Victim dead.
4.	 Management
Antibiotics
•	 All moderate or severe bites, or if patient is at high risk of infection:
•	 Amoxicillin + Clavulanate

–
Child: 20–40 mg/kg/dose (based on amoxicillin component) PO bd

–
Adult: 875 mg/125 mg PO bd

–
Duration: 5 days, extend to 7 days if signs of infection
•	 If penicillin-allergic: doxycycline + metronidazole (consult MO)
Wound care
•	 Clean thoroughly with saline after initial soap/water wash
•	 Do not suture unless wound is large and not grossly contaminated (consult MO)
•	 Update tetanus immunisation (see table)
•	 In rabies-endemic settings, follow national rabies PEP protocols

5.	 Referral/consultation
•	 Urgent referral to MO if:

–
Deep wounds or high-risk site (face, hand, joint)

–
Clinical signs of sepsis or systemic illness

Suspected rabies exposure

–
Consider surgical consult if wound is not healing or needs closure
