# Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP) – Adult

Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP) –
Adult
Recommend
Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) is an infection of preexisting ascitic fluid in
the absence of any secondary intra-abdominal source (e.g. perforated viscus). SBP
typically occurs in patients with end-stage liver disease. Without a high index of
suspicion and early antibiotic treatment, mortality is high.
Background
SBP is caused by translocation of intestinal bacteria into ascitic fluid, most
commonly Escherichia coli, Klebsiella spp., and streptococci. Diagnosis is clinical
and confirmed by ascitic fluid analysis with a PMN leukocyte count ≥250 cells/
microlitre. Patients may present with non-specific or no symptoms, so clinical
suspicion is essential.
1.
May present with
•	 Asymptomatic
•	 Fever
•	 Hypotension
•	 Abdominal pain
•	 Abdominal tenderness
•	 Altered mental status
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Call for help – inform MO
•	 Triage patient and assess vital signs
•	 Start empiric antibiotic therapy to cover intestinal bacteria:

–
Ceftriaxone 2g IV OD, if available

–
OR Ampicillin 1g IV q6h + Gentamicin 240mg IV OD
•	 Continue antibiotics for 5–10 days depending on clinical response
•	 Treat underlying liver disease – if HBsAg-positive, initiate Tenofovir once the
patient is clinically improving
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Assess for fever, abdominal tenderness, or changes in mental status in any
patient with known cirrhosis and ascites
•	 Perform abdominal examination for signs of peritonitis
•	 If trained and resources permit, perform diagnostic paracentesis

–
Diagnosis confirmed if PMN count ≥250 cells/microlitre
•	 Exclude secondary causes of infection (e.g. perforation)

4.	 Management
Empiric antibiotic therapy:
•	 Ceftriaxone 2g IV OD
•	 OR Ampicillin 1g IV q6h + Gentamicin 240mg IV OD
Supportive care:
•	 Monitor blood pressure, temperature, fluid status, and mental status
•	 Continue routine cirrhosis management, including dietary sodium restriction and
diuretics unless contraindicated
•	 If HBV positive, initiate Tenofovir once stable and link to Hepatitis Program
Prophylaxis (in discussion with MO):
Patients at increased risk of SBP (e.g. previous SBP, variceal bleeding, or ascitic fluid
protein &lt;1 g/dL) may benefit from antibiotic prophylaxis with:
•	 Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Septrin) 960mg PO OD
•	 OR Ciprofloxacin 500mg PO OD
5.	 Follow up
•	 Monitor temperature, vitals, abdominal tenderness, and mental status daily while
on treatment
•	 Review antibiotic response after 48–72 hours
•	 Educate patient and family on signs of recurrence (fever, confusion, worsening
abdominal pain)
•	 Ensure linkage to Hepatitis Program if HBsAg-positive
•	 Assess need for long-term prophylaxis with MO
6.	 Referral / consultation
Refer to medical officer or hospital if:
•	 Paracentesis cannot be safely performed
•	 Patient is septic, hypotensive, or deteriorating despite treatment
•	 No clinical improvement within 72 hours
•	 HBV-positive patient needing antiviral therapy or specialist liver care
