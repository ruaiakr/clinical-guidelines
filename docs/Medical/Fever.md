# Fever – Adult

4.	 Management
•	 Address reversible causes:

–
Poor sleep or nutrition: improve rest and diet

–
Overwork: advise rest and gradual return to activity

–
Mild infection: symptomatic treatment
•	 Treat specific conditions if identified:

–
Anaemia: treat per anaemia protocol

–
Diabetes or hypothyroidism: refer to MO

–
Cardiac or respiratory issues: assess and refer as needed

–
Mental health issues: provide support, consider MO review
•	 Reassure if no serious cause found and symptoms are mild
•	 Encourage gradual increase in activity and good sleep hygiene
5.	 Follow up
•	 Reassess within 1–2 weeks or sooner if deterioration
•	 Monitor weight, activity tolerance, mood and energy levels
•	 Repeat full assessment if new symptoms emerge
6.	 Referral/consultation
•	 Refer to MO if:

–
Weakness persists or worsens without clear cause

–
Associated with serious signs (fever, weight loss, breathlessness, neurological
symptoms)

–
Suspected depression or anxiety requiring more support

Fever – Adult
Recommend
•	 Fever in adults may have a wide range of causes. A careful clinical history and
examination can help narrow the differential diagnosis.
•	 If fever persists for >3 weeks without diagnosis, and evaluation has failed to
identify a cause, the condition may be classified as “fever of unknown origin”
(FUO).
•	 Refer to medical officer if patient is clinically unwell, deteriorating, or the cause
of fever remains unclear.
Background
•	 Fever is a common symptom in adults and may reflect infections, malignancies,
autoimmune conditions, or other systemic disorders. Persistent or unexplained
fever, particularly when accompanied by weight loss, night sweats, or
localising symptoms, warrants systematic evaluation. Clinical features may help
distinguish between infectious and non-infectious causes, such as malignancy or
autoimmune disease.
1.
May present with (see table on following pages)
•	 Fever (subjective or documented ≥38°C)
•	 Chills, night sweats, weight loss, anorexia
•	 Localised pain (headache, abdominal pain, back pain, sore throat)
•	 Fatigue, generalised weakness
•	 Cough, hemoptysis
•	 Rash, myalgias, joint pain
•	 Urinary symptoms
•	 Neurological symptoms (confusion, headache, visual changes)
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Assess and stabilise airway, breathing, circulation (ABC) if acutely unwell
•	 Monitor vital signs: temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure,
oxygen saturation
•	 Commence IV fluids if hypotensive or dehydrated
•	 Administer antipyretics (e.g. paracetamol 1g orally every 6 hours if needed)
•	 Isolate patient if suspicion of a contagious infection (e.g. tuberculosis, typhoid,
viral hemorrhagic fever)

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Obtain detailed history:

–
Onset and duration of fever

–
Associated symptoms (see above)

–
Recent travel, exposures (e.g. TB contacts, animals, unsafe food or water)

–
Immunocompromised status (HIV, diabetes, steroid use)

–
Vaccination history

–
Medication history

–
Menstrual and sexual history in women
•	 Perform physical examination:

–
Look for lymphadenopathy, jaundice, rashes, hepatosplenomegaly

–
Cardiac exam: new murmurs (suggestive of endocarditis)

–
Respiratory exam: crackles, reduced air entry (TB or pneumonia)

–
Neurological exam: meningism, confusion

–
Abdominal exam: tenderness, guarding, rebound, ascites

–
Genital and rectal exam as appropriate
4.	 Management
•	 Treat empirically only if a likely diagnosis is made or patient is severely ill
•	 Common syndromes and actions:

–
Suspected malaria: Refer and commence antimalarials if rapid test or thick
smear is positive

–
Suspected pneumonia: Consider antibiotics (e.g. amoxicillin or doxycycline)

–
Suspected TB: Refer for sputum testing and evaluation

–
Suspected UTI: Treat with appropriate antibiotics (e.g. nitrofurantoin or
trimethoprim)

–
Suspected typhoid: Ceftriaxone IV or azithromycin orally

–
Suspected endocarditis: Refer urgently for medical review and blood cultures
•	 Provide supportive care: fluids, nutrition, rest
•	 Monitor progress daily
5.	 Follow up
•	 Review in 2–3 days or sooner if symptoms worsen
•	 If no improvement after 5–7 days of empirical treatment, consult medical officer
•	 Investigate further if fever continues beyond 2 weeks
6.	 Referral/Consultation
Refer to a doctor or hospital if:
•	 Fever >7 days with no clear cause
•	 Severe or rapidly worsening symptoms
•	 Suspected endocarditis, meningitis, or TB
•	 Known malignancy or immunocompromised patient
•	 Need for further laboratory investigations or imaging
