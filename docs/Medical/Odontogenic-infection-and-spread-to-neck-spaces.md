Odontogenic infection and spread to neck
spaces (Kiribati Original Protocol)
Background
Odontogenic infections arise from dental or oral pathology and can lead to serious
systemic complications. If left untreated, these infections can spread to adjacent
tissues, including the neck spaces, leading to severe conditions such as Ludwig’s
Angina. Early detection and prompt management are essential to prevent airway
compromise, sepsis, and other life-threatening consequences.
General Principles
•	 Follow standard infection control protocols to prevent cross-contamination and
ensure patient safety.
•	 Recognize red flags and symptoms that require urgent referral.
•	 Provide early pain management and antibiotics as indicated.
•	 Always ensure airway management is prioritized, especially if neck space
involvement is suspected.
•	 Conduct a full assessment and monitor for any signs of systemic infection or
airway compromise.
1.
May present with
Odontogenic infections may present with the following symptoms, which could
indicate spread to the neck spaces:
•	 Swelling and erythema of the lower face, neck, or jaw.
•	 Severe pain in the affected area.
•	 Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) or painful swallowing.
•	 Trismus (inability to open the mouth fully).
•	 Fever and chills suggesting systemic infection.
•	 Difficulty breathing or hoarseness (suggesting airway compromise).
•	 Swelling of the tongue or floor of the mouth.

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Airway Management: Always assess the airway first. In cases of Ludwig’s Angina,
where airway obstruction is a risk, early intervention to secure the airway is
critical. Consider intubation or emergency surgical intervention if necessary.
•	 Analgesia: Administer appropriate pain relief, considering the severity of the
infection and the patient’s overall condition. Opioid analgesics may be required
for severe pain.
•	 Antibiotics: Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics should be administered
early. Common regimens include a combination of penicillin, metronidazole, or
clindamycin to cover both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria.
•	 Hydration and Fluid Management: Administer IV fluids as necessary to maintain
hydration and support systemic circulation.
•	 Monitoring: Close monitoring of vital signs, including temperature, pulse, and
oxygen saturation, is essential to detect signs of sepsis or airway compromise
early.

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 History: Obtain a detailed history of the symptoms, including the onset and
progression of pain, fever, and swelling. Ask about recent dental procedures or
infections.
•	 Examination: Perform a thorough examination of the oral cavity, including
palpation of the floor of the mouth, submandibular regions, and neck. Assess for
signs of swelling, erythema, and tenderness.
•	 Neurological Examination: Check for signs of infection affecting the cranial
nerves, particularly the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves, which could signal
involvement of deep neck spaces.
•	 Functional Assessment: Evaluate the patient's ability to open the mouth,
swallow, and breathe comfortably. Difficulty opening the mouth or swallowing
could be an indication of severe infection or airway compromise.
