Variceal hemorrhage – Adult
Recommend
Variceal hemorrhage is a life-threatening complication of portal hypertension,
commonly seen in patients with liver cirrhosis from any cause (e.g. chronic hepatitis
B infection, alcohol use). Screening for hepatitis B (HBsAg) is important if the
patient’s status is unknown. Prompt resuscitation, bleeding control, and infection
prevention are essential.
Background
Oesophageal or gastric varices are dilated veins that develop due to portal
hypertension. When these rupture, they can cause massive upper gastrointestinal
bleeding. Early identification and urgent management are critical to reduce
mortality and prevent complications.
Goals of acute therapy are to:
•	 Restore and maintain haemodynamic stability
•	 Restore and maintain adequate oxygenation
•	 Control bleeding
•	 Prevent complications including infection and rebleeding
1.
May present with
•	 Haematemesis (vomiting blood)
•	 Melena (black, tarry stools)
•	 Dizziness or syncope
•	 Signs of shock (e.g. hypotension, tachycardia)
•	 History of liver disease or known cirrhosis
2.	 Immediate management
•	 Call for help – inform MO immediately
•	 Triage patient and assess vital signs
•	 Insert large bore IV access (16G cannula) and begin fluid resuscitation
•	 Aim to maintain BP >90/60 mmHg
•	 Provide oxygen by nasal cannula – maintain oxygen saturation >95%
•	 Consider nasogastric tube (NGT) to clear blood and clots if preparing for
endoscopy
•	 If available, give erythromycin (as a prokinetic) to improve endoscopy visibility
•	 Begin broad-spectrum antibiotics:

–
Ceftriaxone 1g IV OD ± Ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO BD

–
Complete 7 days total antibiotic therapy

3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 Assess ABCs (airway, breathing, circulation) and haemodynamic status
•	 Confirm GI bleeding symptoms and history of liver disease
•	 Perform physical examination for signs of cirrhosis (jaundice, spider naevi,
ascites)
•	 Screen for HBsAg if status unknown
•	 Monitor vital signs continuously
•	 Record baseline haemoglobin if available
4.	 Management
Pharmacologic therapy (if available):
•	 Octreotide 50 mcg IV bolus followed by 50 mcg/hour IV infusion
•	 Continue for 2–5 days following cessation of bleeding
Blood transfusion:
•	 If hypovolaemic or anaemic, transfuse to maintain Hb ≥7 g/dL and &lt;9 g/dL
Endoscopy:
•	 Arrange urgent endoscopy after stabilisation
•	 Should be done within 12 hours of hospital admission
•	 May include variceal band ligation to control bleeding
Nutritional support:
•	 Once haemodynamically stable, wait 24–48 hours post-bleed before resuming
oral or enteral feeds
Chronic management:
•	 If patient is HBsAg-positive, discuss initiation of beta-blocker therapy (e.g.
propranolol) with MO
•	 Refer to Hepatitis Program for antiviral therapy and long-term follow-up
•	 Plan for repeat endoscopy for ongoing variceal surveillance
5.	 Follow up
•	 Monitor for signs of rebleeding (e.g. new haematemesis or melena)
•	 Reassess haemodynamic stability and nutritional intake
•	 Link patient with Hepatitis Program if HBsAg positive
•	 Arrange for long-term follow-up and surveillance endoscopy if available
•	 Educate patient and family on avoiding alcohol and recognising signs of bleeding
