# Trigeminal neuralgia – Adult/Child (Kiribati Original Protocol)

Trigeminal neuralgia – Adult/Child
(Kiribati Original Protocol)
Background
Trigeminal neuralgia is a chronic pain condition involving the trigeminal nerve (5th
cranial nerve).
•	 It causes sudden, severe, sharp, or electric shock-like pain on one side of the face.
•	 Often triggered by light touch, chewing, talking, wind or brushing teeth.
•	 May be idiopathic or due to compression, multiple sclerosis (MS), post-herpetic
neuralgia, or rarely a tumour.
•	 In resource-limited settings, clinical diagnosis and first-line treatment should be
initiated before considering imaging.
•	 See also:

–
Better Health Channel – Trigeminal Neuralgia www.betterhealth.vic.gov.au/
health/conditionsandtreatments/trigeminal-neuralgia

–
https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/trigeminal-neuralgia
Recommend
•	 Always discuss with Medical Officer (MO) if trigeminal neuralgia is suspected.
This condition is rare in children – consider alternative diagnoses in young
patients.
•	 Early treatment improves quality of life and reduces the risk of persistent pain
and weight loss.
•	 If recurrent or resistant to treatment, refer to visiting neurologist or dental
surgeon if available.
1.
May present with
•	 Severe, sharp, stabbing or electric-shock pain in the cheek, jaw, teeth, or
forehead.
•	 Pain occurs on one side only.
•	 Attacks last from a few seconds to 2 minutes, often in clusters.
•	 Pain may be triggered by:

–
Chewing, swallowing, brushing teeth

–
Talking, touching the face, cold wind
•	 No fever, swelling, rash or pus
•	 No facial numbness or weakness (if present → consider another diagnosis)
•	 Atypical or persistent pain, numbness or bilateral symptoms may suggest
another condition (e.g. dental abscess, shingles, sinusitis, MS, tumour).

2.	 Immediate management
•	 Reassure patient and explain condition.
•	 Start first-line medication only after MO review.
•	 Avoid known triggers (e.g. cold wind, chewing on affected side).
•	 Advise to return for review if:

–
Symptoms worsen or do not improve in 1–2 weeks

–
Facial numbness, weakness, or persistent pain occurs
•	 Do not attempt dental extraction unless clear cause identified.
•	 If available, refer to dental or visiting specialist clinic.
3.	 Clinical assessment
•	 History:

–
When did pain start? How long does each episode last?

–
Which part of the face is affected?

–
Any known triggers?

–
Pattern: how often, how intense?

–
Previous similar episodes?

–
Recent dental problems or facial infections?
•	 Examination:

–
Check vital signs

–
Inspect skin for rash or swelling (exclude herpes zoster)

–
Check cranial nerves: any weakness, droop, numbness?

–
Examine teeth and gums

–
Palpate face for tenderness or masses
•	 If neurological signs or persistent pain → MO may refer for imaging (if available)
4.	 Management
•	 First-line medication (after MO review):

–
Carbamazepine
	›
Form: Tablet (100 mg)
	›
Strength: 100 mg
	›
Route: Oral
	›
Dose: Start 100 mg twice daily; increase slowly every 3–7 days
	›
Maximum: Usually 400–1200 mg/day in 2–3 divided doses
	›
Duration: Review after 2 weeks
	›
Contraindications: Hypersensitivity, bone marrow suppression
	›
Safety notes: Monitor for drowsiness, dizziness, rash, low sodium, low white
cell count. Do not stop suddenly.
	›
Blood tests (if available): FBC, electrolytes, LFTs prior and during treatment

•	 If carbamazepine is not tolerated: MO may consider, or amitriptyline if available
•	 Non-medication care

–
Soft diet, avoid cold wind, cover face in wind

–
Offer counselling for chronic pain or mental health impact

–
Monitor for weight loss or sleep disruption
5.	 Follow up
•	 Review within 1–2 weeks to assess symptom control and side effects
•	 If pain is well controlled, continue same dose
•	 If no improvement or poor tolerance, discuss with MO
•	 Ongoing review if long-term therapy is needed
•	 Encourage return for reassessment if:

–
Pain worsens

–
New neurological symptoms develop

–
Unable to eat or sleep

–
Facial numbness or weakness appears
6.	 Referral/consultation
•	 Refer to MO or specialist if:

–
Diagnosis uncertain or persistent pain

–
Numbness or facial weakness present

–
Recurrent episodes not responding to treatment
•	 If available, refer to:

–
Dental services if suspect dental origin

–
ENT or neurology specialist (if visiting or via outreach)

–
Consider telehealth if imaging or specialist opinion needed
•	 If suspected secondary cause (MS, tumour, shingles) → refer to hospital
